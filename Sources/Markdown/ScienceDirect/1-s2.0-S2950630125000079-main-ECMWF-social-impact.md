Benefits are not only derived from short and medium-range fore-
casts. There is an increasing demand for longer lead times in weather
predictions, giving more time to prepare and enabling very early ac-
tions to minimize the economic impact of extreme events. The Red
Cross and Red Crescent Societies are starting to use sub-seasonal fore-
casts to strategize for weather disasters, for instance, to plan where to
move emergency supplies when a tropical cyclone threatens to hit a
region. Furthermore, Forecast-based Financing (FbF)1 enables access
to humanitarian funding for early action based on in-depth forecast
information and risk analysis to anticipate disasters, reducing their
impact, if possible, and human suffering and losses. The allocation
of financial resources is agreed in advance, together with the specific
forecast threshold that triggers the release of those resources. As for
seasonal forecasts, these can allow firms to better assess long-term risks
and, therefore, make better investing decisions. Lemoine and Kapnick
(2024) show that financial traders price the uncertainty generated
by the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)
El Niño and winter outlooks, affecting firms respectively at US$13.4
trillion and US$5.7 trillion, and that a 1% improvement in the skill of
the El Niño outlook reduces firms’ exposure to a one standard devia-
tion shock by $18 billion and induces traders to spend an additional
$2 million hedging the outlook’s news. Improved seasonal forecasts
also contribute to a better understanding of the climate dynamics
which, over time, are expected to produce better climate mitigation
and adaptation policies. Hope (2015) calculates the value of better
information relative to one aspect of climate, i.e. the rise in global
mean temperature that would be caused at the end of 70 years by a

3. ECMWF value added through international collaboration
Weather and climate events impact populations and businesses
locally but must be studied at a global scale. To anticipate phenomena
affecting a region of the world, it is often necessary to understand
the evolution of the state of the atmosphere in other regions. This
encourages international collaboration to facilitate the sharing of data,
codes and information, resulting in the reduction of forecasts’ cost
production, improved forecasting accuracy and a better understanding
of weather and climate patterns. ECMWF has strategically pursued a
wide range of diverse partnerships and relationships with the broad
meteorological and climate community including the NMHSs of Mem-
ber and Co-operating States, international organisations (WMO, EU-
METSAT, ESA), the European Commission, governmental agencies and
commercial companies worldwide, that can take many forms such as
collaboration on research programmes, strategic orientations and par-
ticipation on advisory boards, improvements of NWP, co-development
of products and applications, monitoring of the quality of observations,
training and support in the integration of ECMWF’s output in products
and services targeted to many societal sectors and the public at large. It
is through such entities that most of ECMWF’s socio-economic impact
is manifested.
4. Research work
Research collaborations bring together scientists from different dis-
ciplines, backgrounds and institutions, fostering the exchange of diverse
perspectives, ideas, resources, and expertise. This diversity can spark
innovative approaches and creative solutions to complex problems.
Studies have shown that collaborative innovation activities, capabili-
ties, and knowledge sharing significantly influence innovation perfor-
mance in the public sector (Widyasari and Nasution, 2022) and can
lead to the development of new applications allowing for more robust
and reliable results (Wagner et al., 2015; Shao et al., 2020). Since
ECMWF activities are linked to many aspects of global environmental
monitoring and prediction, research is conducted through collaboration
with most members of this research ecosystem.
To improve NWP, it is paramount to invest in research to guide
the design of the Earth observations network and enhance the use of
observations. In this sense, ECMWF works closely with its Member
States and other partners such as space agencies, primarily EUMETSAT
and ESA, and with significant collaborations with NASA, NOAA, the
Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), the China Meteorological
Administration (CMA), the European Commission and the WMO. This
work enables ECMWF to process and use operationally a large num-
ber of satellite instruments (Fig. 2). ECMWF and EUMETSAT jointly
invest in research on advanced techniques to extract information from
satellite data optimally. For instance, ECMWF’s groundbreaking all-sky
approach to microwave data exploitation has enabled the innovative
extraction of humidity and temperature information in the presence of
clouds and precipitation. It is the first of its kind used in any operational
weather model worldwide. This work has benefited from EUMETSAT
support through its fellowship program and the NWP Satellite Applica-
tion Facility collaboration (including the UK Met Office, Météo-France
and Germany’s DWD) and ESA’s work on the development of satellite

While physics-based NWP models are the backbone of current fore-
casting, ECMWF acknowledges the increasing potential of ML tech-
niques to enhance the process. ML techniques are being investigated
in data assimilation to enhance the accuracy of predictions. Several
studies have shown that ML weather forecasting tools, such as Huawei’s
Pangu Weather, NVIDIA’s FourCastNet and Google DeepMind’s Graph-
Cast, can predict weather with accuracy and faster than traditional
NWP models in medium-range, short-range and ensemble forecasts (Bi
et al., 2023; Charlton-Perez et al., 2024; Ben Bouallègue et al., 2024).
ECMWF entered the arena with the real-time implementation of its
data-driven Artificial Intelligence Forecasting System (wrf). The AIFS
is underpinned by a toolkit, called Anemoi, which provides high-
performance building blocks and pipelines to create and train massively
parallel AI-driven forecast models. AIFS produces highly skilled fore-
casts for upper-air variables, surface weather parameters and tropical
cyclone tracks (Lang et al., 2024). ECMWF runs in real-time AIFS
and other open-source AI models mentioned above to compare their
performances and to document their strengths and weaknesses. ECMWF
is now also developing an ensemble version of AIFS. These devel-
opments are also being used as a basis for collaborations with the
NMHSs of ECMWF’s Member States. In particular, envisaging a wider
use of Anemoi, ECMWF is funding a project led by MET Norway
and MeteoSwiss, with the participation of (currently) other 12 NMHSs
across Europe to build on Anemoi and create data-driven weather
forecasting models at regional or global scale, allowing the NMHSs to
bring datasets and ideas for the architecture configuration, and train
their own models for different applications (ECMWF, 2024a). It is
important to note that ML developments in weather and climate fore-
casting worldwide rely heavily on the use of reanalysis data as learning
datasets. AIFS, Pangu Weather, FourCastNet and GraphCast, as well as
other ML models not mentioned here, use the ERA5 reanalysis, pro-
duced by ECMWF and funded by the Copernicus programme. ECMWF
is already developing the next generation of reanalyses (ERA6), which
will be available at higher resolution. Since future developments of ML
techniques largely depend on improvements of reanalysis products, it is
reasonable to expect that ERA6 will contribute to increasing the speed
and depth with which ML will impact the meteorological sector.

5. Data, forecasts and services
ECMWF is a world leader in global medium-range weather fore-
casting. ECMWF’s ensemble forecast (or ENS) is an ensemble of 51
forecasts designed to indicate the range of possible weather conditions
out to 15 days ahead, including the probability of the occurrence of
high-impact events. According to its Technical Advisory Committee,
ECMWF leads other centres on various medium-range forecast veri-
fication scores, including ensemble scores for precipitation, upper-air
weather parameters, stratospheric forecasts, ocean wave parameters
and spread-skill relationship. This is why the ECMWF forecasts are
sometimes referred to as the ‘‘gold standard’’ of global weather models.
ECMWF regularly updates its forecasting system, typically once a year,
after thorough tests, to improve the forecast skill performance. In 2023,
for instance, ECMWF improved the spatial resolution of ENS from 18 to
9 km, leading to forecast improvements between 2% and 6% for surface
variables and between 1% and 3% for upper-air variables (ECMWF,
2024b).
ECMWF’s medium-range forecasts are extremely important for the
NMHSs of its Member and Co-operating States. All of them use ECMWF’s
data in the medium range as their primary products for their weather
forecasting services if they do not produce such forecasts for capacity
or strategic reasons, or as a complement to their own products and
services. Many of them use ECMWF data as boundary conditions
for their short-range forecasts. For instance, a number of European
countries have weather-based heat health warning systems (HHWS) in
place, which provide graded heat alerts. The majority of the HHWS
are run by the NMHSs. Of the fourteen European countries analysed
by Casanueva et al. (2019), nine used ECMWF raw or derived products,
including the temperature extreme forecast index (EFI), indicating
how extreme predicted temperatures are with respect to the forecast
model climate. HHWS were instrumental to plan interventions ahead
of the heatwave that affected Europe in 2022, which was well pre-
dicted by ECMWF medium-range forecasts (ECMWF, 2022) but still
caused an estimated 61672 deaths (Ballester et al., 2023). Another
example of the use of ECMWF medium-range forecasts and EFI in
particular was in connection with cyclone Filomena which brought ex-
ceptional snow precipitation to the interior of Spain, including Madrid,
in 2021 (ECMWF, 2021). The precipitation was followed by an extreme
cold wave affecting the whole peninsula. Red warnings were set up
by the Spanish NMHS AEMET in most of the central and northeast
quadrant of the peninsula due to the high accumulated precipitation
expected in ECMWF’s ensemble forecast for several consecutive model
runs.
Increasingly, also NMHSs worldwide are using ECMWF data to
initialize their limited area models or to use them directly as fore-
casts in their services. NOAA makes ECMWF forecasts available to the
National Hurricane Centre and includes them in the National Blend
of Models (NOAA, 2024). In 2023 alone, thirteen NMHSs worldwide
subscribed to licences to get ECMWF forecasts in real-time. Many more
NMHS (currently 37, but others are being arranged) of Least Developed
Countries and Small Island Developing States have been granted access
to a special dataset designed to feed initial conditions to limited area
models thanks to an initiative promoted by ECMWF in support of SOFF
to encourage these countries to share observations that improve the
quality of global forecasts, which in turn improve the local forecasts,
thus creating a virtuous circle that eventually benefits all.
ECMWF forecasts in the medium range are also used by private
weather companies and other enterprises to provide specialized ser-
vices in sectors such as energy, transportation, logistics, insurance,
intelligence, and sporting, as well as weather applications for public
use. The number of commercial licences for the use of ECMWF data
rose from 103 in 2011 to 392 in 2023. For instance, Windy is a
Czech company enabling 800,000 users per day worldwide (CAMS,
2024) to access comprehensive weather forecasting services using so-

6. Infrastructure, software tools, applications and services
Underpinning all ECMWF activities are the computing infrastruc-
ture and software tools (including HPCF, software applications, cloud
etc.) allowing research to be carried out, vast amounts of data to be
analysed, forecasting data to be produced and disseminated in real-
time and collaborations/partnerships to be sustained. In return, many
research activities provide feedback on the computing infrastructure to
help improving its capacity or creating new applications for weather
forecasting operations.
The HPCF is essential for the operational and research activities of
ECMWF. Data assimilation, dynamical equation solving, parametrizat-
ion, and ensemble components of climate monitoring and weather/
atmospheric composition forecasting are computationally intensive
processes. The HPCF allows for faster model simulations, increased res-
olutions (Fig. 4), processing of vast ensembles of climate and weather
scenarios, leading to more accurate, reliable and timely predictions.
75% of the capacity of the HPCF is dedicated to ECMWF’s activities
(25% for the Centre’s time-critical operations and 50% for its research
activities).
In 2017 ECMWF commissioned a socio-economic benefit internal
report to Citizing to evaluate the incremental benefits that would be
delivered by different levels of HPCF investment, concluding that the
benefits deriving from an option that was considered ‘‘intermediate’’
were estimated at around five billion pounds over the envisaged four-
year contract period. This estimate was capturing only the incremental
value to products and services enabled by the HPCF upgrade and not
the underlying value present before such upgrade and did not at-
tempt to capture the value deriving from the overall ECMWF activities
described in this paper.
In October 2022, ECMWF started to use its new HPCF provided via
a service contract with Atos and featuring over one million processors
(1015
spread across 4 clusters and a performance of about 30 petaflops
or a million billion calculations per second). The increased medium-
range ENS resolution from 18 km to 9 km implemented in 2023 thanks
to the new HPCF, led to a 2.5 factor increase in the volume of data
processed, compared to the previous cycle.
ECMWF makes 25% of its HPC capacity available to its Member
States for their time-critical operations and research activities, includ-
ing for NWP application development and production of software, pre-
and post-processing, computational research, and to be used as part
of their disaster recovery plans. In 2020, for instance, after a strong
earthquake destroyed the headquarters of DHMZ (the Croatian NMHS)
in Zagreb, the time-critical operations could be rapidly backed up on
ECMWF HPCF and IT infrastructure, allowing the NMHS to continue
operating to provide forecasts and other services to the nation.
ECMWF’s HPCF is also used to support the WMO. The South-East
European Multi-Hazard Early Warning Advisory System (SEE-MHEWS-
A), for instance, is an initiative started in 2014 by WMO, designed
to prepare for and respond effectively to natural hazards and extreme
weather events by improving forecasting and warning provisions across
19 countries in Southeast Europe (WMO, 2024). The platform, im-
plemented using ECMWF’s HPCF and the European Weather Cloud
(EWC), currently allows forecasters from the countries involved to work
collaboratively to identify potential hazards and impacts, especially
when impending weather events may affect multiple countries. Since
the launch of the SEE-MHEWS-A initiative, data exchange in the region
has increased by 50%, and further improvement is expected, including
incorporating other types of data, such as hydrological observations and
radar data (WMO, 2024).

7. Conclusions and new ECMWF strategy
More than ever, society is dependent on accurate weather and
climate data and the ability of research institutions, governmental
agencies, commercial companies and other players to integrate them
into their own processes to provide actionable information to influence
decisions that can have a positive, profound and long-lasting societal
impact. ECMWF, with its products, infrastructure and services and
the strong, deep and far-reaching links with its Member States and,
more generally, the meteorological and environmental sciences ecosys-
tem, is uniquely positioned to provide a fundamental contribution
to addressing the environmental challenges ahead. The evolution of
the landscape, as mentioned in section 1, however, required a new
long-term strategy, which was developed in 2024 to cover the period
2025–2034 (ECMWF, 2025). It is paramount that ECMWF remains a
leader in global NWP, prioritizing high-impact weather forecasting and
integration of ML in scientific developments. ECMWF needs to expand
activities where benefits for the efficient operation of the European
partners can be realized, innovating at the cutting edge of physical,
computational and data science for environmental monitoring and pre-
diction and exploiting data-driven methods anchored on physics-based
modelling. Until 2022 ECMWF, like many other traditional players
in the meteorological field, considered that the main disruptive im-
pact of AI/ML would be to accelerate or improve components of the
existing NWP setup (e.g. in data assimilation, observation operators,
data monitoring etc.). ECMWF has since embraced AI/ML techniques
also as an alternative to physics-based simulations, in particular but
not exclusively for the forecast itself, with the first operational imple-
mentation of its AIFS model in February 2025. This first operational
implementation of an ML model in our community paves the way to
future applications of AI/ML for ensemble forecasting at all ranges and
extensions to the Earth system, as well as developments in predicting
the weather directly from observations and the development of a
foundation model.
The difficulty and complexity of accessing large amounts of data
are often highlighted by users in their interactions with ECMWF. While
network, hardware and software updates are regularly implemented
to address these issues, their benefits are offset by the increase in the
availability of new and improved datasets, ever-increasing resolutions
and ever-expanding and demanding user base. ECMWF will, therefore,
continue its efforts in developing cloud services and maximization of
the benefits deriving from proximity to data, portability, interoperabil-
ity, and federation. Greater emphasis will be put on collaborations,
primarily with the European Meteorological Infrastructure, through
existing and new mechanisms, particularly in new and challenging
scientific and technological domains, by co-developing software tooling
and applications that can be exploited by the wider community and at
the same time contribute to building expertise. In the spirit of greater
collaboration and to break the barriers to a broader uptake of its
products, in the new strategy, ECMWF is accelerating the adoption
of open policies for data and software, recognizing that many other
players in the meteorological and climatological sectors have already
demonstrated some of the substantial benefits that can be derived.
