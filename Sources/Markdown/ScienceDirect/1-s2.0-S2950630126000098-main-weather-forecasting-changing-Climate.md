3. ML weather forecasting works
The performance of ML-based forecasts is now well documented in
the peer-reviewed literature. Deterministic ML models have achieved
medium-range skill comparable to state-of-the-art NWP, with anomaly
correlation coefficients for upper-air fields surpassing or matching those
10–15
of traditional models out to days (Price et al., 2025; Lang et al.,
2024). At ECMWF, the Artificial Intelligence Forecasting System (AIFS),
implemented in 2025, produces medium-range forecasts using deep
neural networks trained on reanalysis and operational data (Lang et al.,
2024). AIFS runs alongside the physics-based model of the Integrated
Forecasting System (IFS), providing deterministic and ensemble proba-
bilistic forecasts that match or exceed the skill of traditional models in
many metrics (Lang et al., 2024; Moldovan et al., 2025; Lang et al.,
2025). Examples of performance scores are provided in Fig. 1, for a
whole season.
Such improvements are highly significant in a field where advances
are hard-won and have huge societal impact. For example, extending the
useful forecast range by even a single day can provide earlier warnings
for extreme weather, improve disaster preparedness, and enhance
decision-making in sectors such as agriculture and energy (Venuti et al.,
2025).

typically have poorer resolution in space and time than physics-based
systems, they can be under-dispersive in particular in the case of pre-
cipitation, and crucially the range of output products is currently still

5. Outlook
The integration of machine learning into weather forecasting is
reshaping the field, enabling new forms of collaboration, efficiency, and
ECMWF’s
scientific discovery. experience demonstrates that ML
weather forecasts can be responsibly embedded within established
workflows, delivering tangible improvements in forecast skill. As ML
models become standard tools in meteorology, continued emphasis on
transparency, collaboration, and rigorous evaluation will be essential to
realise their full potential.
Presently, as explained in this article, ML augments, not replaces
NWP: the ML prediction step relies on the analysis produced by the
physics-based system and the training depends on re-analysis datasets.
This obviously creates dependencies and large organisational demands.
The exact balance of which system runs routinely in operations and
which system is used as a reference and complement is certainly not
fixed, and indeed will very likely evolve over time.
Looking ahead, the expansion of ML methods to coupled Earth sys-
tem modelling, the development of new architectures, and the adoption
of continual learning paradigms are likely to further enhance forecast
(Ban˜o-Medina
skill and adaptability et al., 2025). ECMWF is exploring
AI-DOP (McNally et al., 2024; Alexe et al., 2024), an encompassment of
data-assimilation and forecasting in a single model that learns to fore-
cast directly from observations (McNally et al., 2024; Alexe et al., 2024).
This has shown skilful behaviour, with an array of teams across the globe
exploring similar approaches (Sun et al., 2024; Allen et al., 2025; Ni
et al., 2025).
Broadly, there appears to be three possible endgames for the AI/ML
revolution:
1. Long-term symbiosis of physics-based and ML forecasting systems
with complementary strengths.
2. ML forecasting dominates real-time predictions, but physical model
data remaining a key ingredient in training these models and phys-
ical model developments driving forward ML forecasting skill.
3. ML forecasting dominates real-time predictions with skill improve-
ments being driven by other sources, e.g. observations.
Whilst a future where ML forecasts are the dominant real-time sys-
tem currently appears plausible, there are significant milestones to
achieve this goal: demonstrating superiority for all forecast applications,
covering all products and accurately initialising from observations are
three key milestones. For the foreseeable future, investment in both
physics-based and ML systems appears the optimal path for a robust and
accurate weather forecasting ecosystem.
The careful adoption of machine learning in weather prediction
stands to benefit both science and society, extending the boundaries of
what is possible in forecasting. Data-driven methods are now being
successfully deployed in operations for weather forecasting in the short
to medium ranges and have started to show potential in the longer
seasonal ranges (Kent et al., 2025). Improving weather forecasts through
ML methods in the sub-seasonal to seasonal ranges will be particularly
important for the adaptation to climate change.
