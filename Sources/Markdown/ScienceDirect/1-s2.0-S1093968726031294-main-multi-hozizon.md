recommended.

3. Study site and dataset
The monitoring site investigated in this study is located near the
prefectural boundary between Tokushima and Kochi Prefectures, Japan.
The instrumented slope is situated on a valley side within mountainous
terrain. It extends approximately 80 m along the slope direction, and
about 40 m across the slope. The slope gradient within the monitored
32◦
area is approximately Prior field and remote-sensing observations at
this site have suggested that the slope exhibits persistent landslide ac-
tivity. Comparison of aerial images taken over successive years shows
surface-scale collapse features on the moving mass, as well as morpho-
logical changes that are consistent with ongoing downslope deforma-
tion. Kinematically, the landslide body can be divided along the slope-

The training and test periods both correspond to relatively stable

model should therefore be interpreted as a tilt response prediction

may no longer hold. Validation against historical failure-event records is

Future work will extend the state-space formulation by incorporating

5.4. Cross-Site transferability
Validation in this study is based on a single site. Three structural
elements carry over without modification: the two-state decomposition
(hwet driving hinst), the power-softplus observation function, and the
Delayed-EKF training procedure.
All 8 learnable parameters must be re-estimated from site-local data,
as they encode drainage rate, coupling strength, dissipation, and noise
levels governed by local geology, hydrology, and sensor geometry.
Because the model has only 8 parameters, each with outsized influence,
conventional transfer-learning strategies are inapplicable. Deployment
requires tilt sensors and a rain gauge, records covering at least 10
distinct rainfall events, and L-BFGS-B optimization of the 8 parameters.
Periodic re-estimation is recommended as observation data accumulate.
The prediction outputs support an operational early-warning work-
flow combining two information sources: real-time monitoring, where
sustained elevation of observed tilt increments signals a transition from
primary to secondary creep; and SSM forecasts, which predict the tilt
increment and 90% PI over the next H hours, providing advance
warning before a critical response materializes. The warning level is
determined jointly by both sources. A missed alarm occurs when the
slope enters accelerated creep but neither source triggers a warning; a
false alarm is the converse. Quantitative evaluation of alarm rates re-
quires labeled event records from extended deployment, which is a di-
rection for future work.
6. Conclusions
This study developed a physics-based framework for multi-horizon
prediction of rainfall-induced slope response by combining a two-
state-space model (SSM) with an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). Using
tilt-monitoring data from a slope site in Japan, the proposed framework
was systematically evaluated against a tree-based ensemble model
(XGBoost) and a sequence model (LSTM). The main conclusions are as
follows.
1. First, explicit modeling of physical latent states is central to
improving predictive performance in this low-dimensional moni-
toring setting. With only 8 learnable parameters, the proposed SSM
consistently outperformed both baselines across forecast horizons
from 1 to 24 h for both dX and dY, achieving a peak R² of 0.857 for dX
at the 12 h horizon. This result suggests that, under rainfall-driven
monitoring conditions, predictive performance depends less on
model size than on whether the underlying physical process is rep-
resented in a structurally meaningful way.
2. Second, analytical covariance propagation within the EKF provided
more reliable uncertainty estimates than those of the compared
baselines. The 90% prediction-interval coverage of the SSM

expanded adaptively with forecast horizon. By contrast, XGBoost
showed clear under-coverage at long horizons, and the residual-
based intervals of LSTM did not adequately reflect horizon-
dependent uncertainty. These results indicate that uncertainty
quantification is not merely an auxiliary output, but an essential
component of slope early-warning models.
