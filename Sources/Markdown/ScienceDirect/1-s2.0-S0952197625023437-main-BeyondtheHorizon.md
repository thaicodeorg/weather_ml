Artificial Intelligence (AI) is particularly prominent in overcoming
challenges across diverse domains, especially post-processing tools for
correcting systematic errors in NWP output data, enhancing resolution,
and accounting for topographic effects (Aznarte and Siebert, 2017;
Buehner et al., 2010). Besides, AI provides an opportunity to assess
predictability considering the uncertainty of ensemble forecasting
(Wilks, 2002; Foley et al., 2012; Mallet et al., 2009) and addressing
problems related to extreme events such as hailstorms, gale storms, or
cyclones (McGovern et al., 2017; Williams et al., 2008; Herman and
Schumacher, 2018). Although innovative methodologies are devel-
oping, NWP simulations remain relevant, and their impact on improving
output quality is significant because AI-based models work based on the
input data (Wilks, 1995). All these issues, combined with the availability
of petabyte-scale climate and weather data on a global scale and the
recent reduction in computational demands for Machine Learning (ML)
and Deep Learning (DL) models, have sparked interest in integrating
AI-based methods into weather and climate modeling among weather
scientists and AI researchers. Many of ML algorithms that are used in the
core of AI-based weather forecasting models help in correcting the
systematic errors that arise out of NWP outputs and improve spatial and
temporal resolution. AI does not replace conventional models but en-
hances them with the ability to model the complexities governing at-
mospheric dynamics.
Several recent review papers have explored the intersection of AI and
weather forecasting, each contributing valuable insights but with
notable limitations in scope and coverage. Camps-Valls et al. (2025)
focus on the use of AI for modeling and understanding extreme weather
and climate events, emphasizing explainability, ethical concerns, and
stakeholder engagement. Waqas et al. (2024) provide a technically
detailed survey on integrating AI into NWP systems, particularly in areas
such as data assimilation, and post-processing. Similarly, Zhang et al.
(2025) present a taxonomy of ML techniques and discuss their general
applicability in weather forecasting. While informative, these reviews
tend to concentrate on specific aspects or applications without deliv-
ering a comprehensive, systematic, and comparative evaluation of
recent AI-based weather forecasting models. Notably, they do not
analyze state-of-the-art (SOTA) models such as FengWu, ClimaX,
Pangu-Weather, FourCastNet, GraphCast, GenCast, and the Artificial
Intelligence Forecasting System (AIFS), nor assess their performance
across real-world forecasting scenarios. In contrast, this review provides
a broad analysis of more than 40 AI-based weather forecasting models
developed primarily after 2015. It offers a unified examination of
various critical aspects, including model architectures, training and
evaluation datasets, predictand parameters, extreme event forecasting,
spatiotemporal scales, lead times, performance metrics, overfitting, data

2. ML models
This section focuses on the different ML and DL methods used in AI-
based weather forecasting models. Depending on their complexity and
sophistication, these models are categorized into four classes including
traditional ML models, general Neural Networks (NNs), modern DL
approaches, and Graph NNs (GNNs).
2.1. Traditional ML methods
Studying the advantages and disadvantages of traditional models
used in AI-based weather forecasting research starts with Standard
Regression (SR) methods, especially Linear Regression (LR), which is the
simplest and most understandable method, but it may not handle non-
linear characteristics in the data well (Kuligowski and Barros, 1998;
McCrea et al., 2005; Hewage et al., 2021). Kernel Ridge Regression
(KRR) is the next method that is a regularization extension intended for
regression tasks with both positive and negative aspects when dealing
with highly non-linear data sets (Vovk, 2013; Lagomarsino-Oneto et al.,
2023). As for K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), it is known for its simplicity
and natural ability to capture localized, changing relationships. How-
ever, computational demands are high, especially with larger datasets,
while interpretability is limited (Sharma et al., 2014; Taunk et al.,
2019). Random Forest (RF) provides a better solution than others that
handle non-linearity and can tell us which predictors are important in
prediction tasks. Despite this advantage, there are several challenges,
including the existence of highly imbalanced data sets as well as

2.2. Surge in NNs
In recent years, researchers have resorted to NNs as a way of cir-
cumventing the limitations of traditional models. NNs are well-suited for
parallel computation and adept at handling complex relationships. They
have good scalability with regard to computational resources especially
when dealing with big data unlike many other traditional models that
suffer from computational bottlenecks as data size increases. In this
paper, general NNs have been classified into four groups namely shallow
NN, multilayer and deep NN, basic Convolutional NNs (CNNs), and basic
Recurrent NNs (RNNs). Shallow NNs can be trained quickly and
implemented easily. Hence, they cannot capture intricate relationships
of complex data that could be used in high-level feature abstraction
tasks. In comparison with traditional architectures, a multi-layer model
is more powerful in its ability to learn complex non-linear relations.

These kinds of NN models that were used in previous studies include
Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) and Deep Belief Network for Precipitation
Forecast (DBNPF). A major disadvantage of MLPs is that they have a
shallow architecture, which consists of only a few hidden layers
(Ramchoun et al., 2016). This means they cannot learn and represent
detailed hierarchical features in data automatically. In the context of
DBNPF, the architecture involves the use of Restricted Boltzmann Ma-
chines (RBMs) for unsupervised pre-training and a top layer with
backpropagation (Zhang et al., 2017). The incorporation of RBMs and
the specific training approach make it different from a traditional MLP.
Deep NNs like DBNPF that have deeper architectures are good at
capturing complicated interdependencies and getting out higher-order
properties like these. However, they tend to overfit and demand
intense computational resources for training. More specifically, the
problem of needing large amounts of data or inputs, possible vanishing
or exploding gradients during training, and increased computational
complexity poses some challenges for them. Therefore, deep NNs are
generally used at the heart of special NN models to optimal use of them.
The CNNs try to capture spatial hierarchies and variability in the input
data (Taye, 2023). On the other hand, RNNs suit sequential data and
time-series forecasting more appropriately. Still, these models face is-
sues such as vanishing gradient problems as well as computational in-
tensity, which is unparallelizable (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997;

2.3. Advanced NN methods
Advanced NN methods are diverse and tailored to address specific
challenges across various applications. Among the more sophisticated
CNN methods, U-shaped network (U-Net), originally designed for
biomedical image segmentation, utilizes a specialized architecture to
achieve precise segmentation tasks. Segmented NN (SegNet), on the
other hand, focuses on semantic segmentation, employing an encoder-
decoder architecture with skip connections to preserve spatial infor-
mation effectively (Larraondo et al., 2019). Temporal Convolutional
Network (TCN) is designed for sequential data processing, capturing
long-range dependencies efficiently (Hewage et al., 2021). Capsule
Networks (CapsNets) introduce capsules as fundamental units, excelling
in tasks involving hierarchical relationships (Chattopadhyay et al.,
2020). Lastly, Residual Networks (ResNets) mitigate the vanishing
gradient problem through skip connections, enabling the construction of
deeper network architectures (Nguyen et al., 2023a, 2023b). In the
realm of RNNs, advanced methods offer enhanced capabilities for
sequence modeling and analysis. Bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM) captures
contextual information from both past and future sequences, facilitating
a more comprehensive understanding of temporal dynamics (Hewage
et al., 2021). Spatiotemporal Stacked-LSTM (SS-LSTM) specializes in
analyzing spatiotemporal data, effectively modeling complex temporal

Transformer-based architectures have revolutionized sequence pro-

Vision Transformer (ViT) uses self-attention mechanisms for sequence

tailored for volumetric data analysis, capturing spatial dependencies

Recurrent NN (SwinRNN) integrates transformer blocks into recurrent
architectures, enhancing sequential modeling capabilities. Swin
transformer-based Variational RNN (SwinVRNN) extends SwinRNN to
incorporate variational inference, enabling probabilistic modeling of
sequential data (Hu et al., 2023). Adaptive Fourier Neural Operator
(AFNO) leverages Fourier analysis to learn adaptive basis functions for
efficient representation of complex data patterns (Pathak et al., 2022).
Cross-Modal Fusion Transformer (CMFT) is designed for multimodal
data fusion, effectively integrating information from diverse sources
through attention mechanisms (Chen et al., 2023b). Autoencoder
methods offer unsupervised learning techniques for feature extraction
and data compression. Sparse AutoEncoder model (SAE) introduces
sparsity constraints to encourage the learning of robust and compact
representations (Zhang et al., 2017). Autoencoders-based Deep NN with
SVR (SVRDNN) incorporate SVR into deep autoencoder architectures for
enhanced performance in regression tasks (Liu et al., 2015). Generative
methods, including Variational AutoEncoders (VAEs) and diffusion
models, enable the probabilistic generation of data samples. VAEs are a
type of autoencoder that incorporates probabilistic techniques for
generating data. Unlike traditional autoencoders, which learn deter-
ministic mappings from input to latent space, VAEs learn a probabilistic
distribution over latent space (Chen et al., 2023c; Kwok and Qi, 2021).
Diffusion models employ iterative diffusion processes to model data
distribution effectively, generating realistic and diverse samples. In a
diffusion model, the initial data sample is gradually transformed into a
new sample through a series of diffusion steps. At each step, noise is
added to the data sample, leading to a diffusion process that gradually
spreads the data distribution (Hua et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024). Table .2

Weather forecasting involves analyzing and predicting complex,
nonlinear, and high-dimensional atmospheric systems. To effectively
address this complexity, data-driven techniques such as Proper
Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) and Dynamic Mode Decomposition
(DMD) are frequently employed (Manning and Baldick, 2019; Sharma
et al., 2024; Teixeira et al., 2025). POD identifies dominant spatial
structures such as pressure systems, temperature fields, and wind pat-
terns, allowing the system to be represented in a reduced-order form.
This preserves key features while substantially lowering computational
costs, enabling faster and more efficient simulations (My Ha et al., 2008;
Wang et al., 2023). DMD captures the temporal evolution of coherent
atmospheric structures by extracting dominant dynamic modes along
with their growth or decay rates. Operating in a model-free and
data-driven manner, DMD can be applied directly to observational or
simulation data, making it especially valuable for analyzing transient
phenomena such as atmospheric blocking, wave propagation, and storm
development (Mansouri et al., 2023; Sofi and Oseledets, 2024; Palma
et al., 2024). By reducing data complexity and isolating essential pat-
terns, POD and DMD enhance the efficiency and accuracy of subsequent
learning processes when integrated with traditional or DL models.

Some of the current AI-based models are specialized in predicting a
few meteorological parameters (up to 3 parameters), called limited-

variable models, while some others focus on multi-variable fore-

perception affected by increased relative humidity or the pollutant

4.2. Lead time and temporal resolution
According to their lead time, weather forecasting models can be
divided into short-term, medium-term, and long-term forecasts. Short-
term forecasts are more focused on the current state of the atmo-
sphere, which includes weather conditions from within an hour up to
three days (Frnda et al., 2022). Medium-term predictions are able to go
beyond this time frame and generally describe typical weather condi-
tions for a period of three days up to 10 days (Bengtsson, 1984). In
contrast, long-term forecasting covers much longer periods, ranging
from two weeks to months, years, or even decades. Another important
aspect of weather forecasting models is temporal resolution. High tem-
poral resolution enables the models to capture and understand rapid
changes in weather variables. This is particularly important for phe-
nomena such as thunderstorms, where conditions can evolve quickly.
4.3. Spatial dimension
Various weather forecasting models focus on at different spatial
scales including a city, a country, a continent, or whole of the world.
Generally, weather models at the local scale have finer spatial resolu-
tions. Therefore, they can explain how local geography and topography
might influence weather patterns in subtle ways. However, most of the
local AI-based weather forecasting models focus on meteorological
stations that provide low spatial resolution. Global models is based on
atmospheric conditions occurring globally, thus having wider coverage
but not at finer spatial resolutions as regional models do. Consequently,
global forecasts may not capture localized weather events as precisely as
regional forecasts. Despite this limitation, global models play a critical
role in comprehending large-scale atmospheric dynamics and supplying
essential boundary conditions for regional models. Table .6 shows
objective parameters and spatiotemporal specifications of the models.
In earlier models, the focus was primarily on limited-variable pre-
diction equivalent up to three variables such as precipitation amount,
temperature, and wind speed. However, in recent models, there has been
a shift towards predicting a greater number of parameters, indicating a
transition towards more comprehensive models. Though extreme
weather has started receiving some attention, the capability of most of
the current models has not been studied in extreme weather prediction.
Nonetheless, some models, such as the one developed by Lam et al.
(2023), focus on different types of severe weather situations, including
tropical cyclones, atmospheric rivers, and high temperatures. Spatial
scale has expanded significantly over time. The earlier models are
focused on particular places or stations while the most recent ones
include bigger areas or even a global scale. Prediction lead time remains
an important feature of weather forecasting. The majority of the possible
solutions concentrate mainly on short-term predictions, which usually
range from a few hours up to three days; however, there is a growing
interest in extending prediction horizons to medium and long-term
forecasts, especially due to the emphasis on extreme weather events.
Temporal resolution varies across models, with some offering hourly
updates while others provide daily or even monthly forecasts.

Errors arising during data assimilation generally fall into two cate-
gories: inaccuracies in initial conditions and deficiencies in the fore-
casting models themselves. Traditional assimilation techniques, such as
the Kalman filter, typically treat these errors as additive, uncorrelated
(white) noise with zero mean. Although these assumptions simplify the
error modeling process, they often do not hold true in reality. For
instance, operational forecasting centers like ECMWF have relaxed some
of these assumptions, using multiplicative error models to better capture
error characteristics (Buizza et al., 1999). The zero-mean error
assumption is especially limiting because it overlooks systematic biases
in models that can vary across space, time, and weather conditions (Dee,
2005). Many NWP systems do not explicitly model these biases, opting
instead for indirect corrections like debiasing residuals or inflating
background error covariances. Techniques such as variational bias
(Aulign´e
correction et al., 2007) and background error inflation
(Houtekamer and Zhang, 2016) address some of these issues. A more
comprehensive method is the Weak-Constraint 4D-Var (WC-4D-Var),
which extends the assimilation framework by explicitly accounting for
ECMWF’s
model error tendencies (Tremolet, 2006). Integrated Fore-
casting System (IFS) Cycle 47R1 recently demonstrated operational
improvements, particularly in reducing biases in the stratosphere, using
this approach (Laloyaux et al., 2020).
In recent years, AI-based data assimilation methods have emerged
that enhance traditional approaches by employing ML and DL to better
identify and correct model errors and biases. These hybrid methods
integrate physics-based models with ML to boost both precision and
adaptability. ML is often utilized through surrogate modeling, where
NNs approximate complex components of the system, lowering
computational demands while preserving critical dynamics. Unlike
conventional data assimilation, which depends on physical assumptions
and statistical models, AI-based methods learn directly from historical
data and simulation outputs. Examples include RNNs (Park, 2010) and
ResNets (Brajard et al., 2020), which are designed to capture the un-
derlying system dynamics or response functions (Fablet et al., 2018;
Long et al., 2018; Bocquet et al., 2020). A frequent challenge with
AI-based assimilation methods is the unrealistic assumption of complete
observability, whereas actual observations are often sparse and noisy.
To overcome this, Brajard et al. (2020) proposed an approach that al-
ternates between the model training and assimilation updates, allowing
both the surrogate model and state estimates to be refined simulta-
neously. Bocquet et al. (2020) further developed this idea within a
Bayesian framework to better handle uncertainty. Additionally, CNNs
applied across multiple dimensions have been used to process
image-based observational data, enhancing feature extraction for
assimilation tasks such as estimating tropical cyclone intensity (Lee
et al., 2019).
Despite these promising advances, AI-based data assimilation faces
several challenges. These methods can be computationally demanding,
sensitive to initial conditions, and prone to instability. A common
strategy to address these issues is to combine DL components with
existing physics-based models rather than completely replacing them
(Pathak et al., 2018; Watson, 2019; Casas et al., 2020; Bonavita and
Laloyaux, 2020). Nevertheless, problems persist, including the need for
large datasets with labeled training examples and the difficulty of
reconciling differences in spatial and temporal resolutions between
model data and observations.

traditional NWP systems. Conventional forecasting relies heavily on a

models, treating weather forecasting as an initial value problem that

Current AI-based models, while promising, still depend on this numer-

Data-driven models Aardvark have been developed to overcome
these limitations by replacing the entire NWP pipeline with a single
data-driven system. Data-driven models directly learn to map raw
observational data to global and local weather forecasts without relying

cally reduces computational costs and complexity, enabling much faster
and more flexible forecasting while maintaining or even improving ac-
curacy compared to operational NWP systems.
Aardvark Weather is a groundbreaking DL system that redefines
weather forecasting by eliminating reliance on traditional physics-based
models at deployment. Instead, it learns a direct mapping from obser-
vational data to forecasts. The system comprises three integrated mod-
ules: an encoder that estimates the initial atmospheric state from
heterogeneous data both on and off the grid, a processor that autore-
gressively generates global forecasts, and a decoder that converts these
into local predictions for weather stations. At its core, Aardvark uses a
vision transformer for encoding and processing, paired with a light-
weight convolutional decoder for local outputs. Aardvark produces
global forecasts for variables like temperature, wind, humidity, and
◦
pressure on a 1.5 grid, and local forecasts for 2-m temperature and 10-

Training mirrors deployment: the encoder is pretrained on raw obser-

cessor is trained on ERA5 inputs and outputs, and the decoder is trained
using model outputs against raw station data. Final end-to-end fine-
tuning optimizes for specific regions or variables. Remarkably, Aardvark
uses only about 8 % of the observational data required by traditional
numerical weather prediction systems, yet matches or surpasses them in
accuracy and lead time. It ingests diverse data sources, including in situ
measurements from stations, ships, and radiosondes, as well as satellite-
based remote sensing like scatterometers and infrared sounders. These
inputs, structured as gridded and non-gridded formats, are transformed
into an initial atmospheric state via the encoder. ERA5 reanalysis plays a
key role in training, helping the model learn and correct observational

biases without explicit bias correction. One of most trans-
formative features is its speed and efficiency. It delivers forecasts in
seconds using only a few Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), compared to
the thousands of supercomputer node hours required by conventional
NWP systems. This makes rapid, scalable, and cost-effective deployment
possible, including in regions with limited computational resources
(Allen et al., 2025).
One of the most important limitations of these models is under-
performing in predicting extreme weather events because such condi-
tions are underrepresented in their training data, leading to potential
underestimation of risks. Finally, the evaluation of these AI models is
often incomplete and lacks standardized benchmarks, underscoring the
need for improved methods to enhance their credibility and robustness.

7.1. Challenges
Several challenges persist in AI-based weather forecasting models in
achieving optimal performance and accuracy.
7.1.1. Limited, low-quality, low resolution data and small-scale forecasting
The scarcity of high-quality, high-resolution historical meteorolog-
ical data poses a significant obstacle to the effective application of AI in
weather forecasting. Limited and low-quality datasets reduce the reli-
ability of AI models by undermining both the training process and the
validation of forecasting algorithms. The success of AI-based models
fundamentally depends on training data and initial conditions that are
diverse and accurate. Biases and inaccuracies often arise from uneven
data coverage or changes in measurement techniques over time and
space. For example, historical meteorological records may contain in-
consistencies due to evolving sensor technologies or shifting environ-
mental conditions. These factors diminish the representativeness of
training data. Therefore, ensuring that AI-based models are trained on
data that are high-quality, diverse, and representative is essential to
reducing errors and improving forecast reliability.
Current SOTA AI-based weather models primarily rely on nearly four
decades of ECMWF ERA5 data for both training and validation. For
single-step forecasting, these models use ERA5 data as the initial con-
dition. In multi-step forecasting, the process begins with ERA5 data at
the initial time. The model then recursively uses its own previous pre-
0.25◦
dictions, which are generated at the same (~30 km) resolution, as
input for subsequent time steps. This autoregressive approach represents
a significant advancement. Nevertheless, the relatively coarse resolution
models’
of ERA5 limits the ability to resolve small-scale atmospheric
phenomena, particularly convection. Accurately predicting convection
remains a major challenge because the ~30 km resolution is insufficient
to capture convective processes, which are key drivers of severe weather
events such as thunderstorms (Gustafsson et al., 2018; Buschow et al.,
2024). Without adequate resolution, forecasts struggle to represent
small-scale dynamics that influence localized weather outcomes. This
limitation highlights the urgent need to incorporate high-resolution,
high-quality humidity data, at least in initial conditions, and ideally
throughout all training and validation stages. Such data are critical for
improving small-scale weather forecasts.
Some studies have applied downscaling techniques to enhance the
spatial resolution of forecast results (Nguyen et al., 2023b; Liu et al.,
2024). However, research indicates that simply increasing spatial reso-
lution does not necessarily improve model performance when critical
parameters like humidity remain inaccurate (Ducrocq et al., 2002).
Therefore, improving humidity data accuracy in ERA5 through assimi-
lation of high-quality, high-resolution tropospheric observations from
advanced technologies such as remote sensing techniques is essential.
Integrating these data will enhance key parameters needed for
convective-scale modeling and ultimately lead to better AI-based
weather forecasts.
Current AI models typically rely on NWP outputs and their data
assimilation pipelines, limiting their ability to fully replace conventional
forecasting. Emerging data-driven models, such as Aardvark system,
demonstrate promising results by learning direct mappings from raw
observational data to forecasts, thereby reducing computational costs
and complexity. However, these models face challenges including
underperformance in predicting extreme weather events, reliance on
ERA5 data for training, and the lack of standardized evaluation
benchmarks.
7.1.2. Explainability
AI-based models, particularly those used in weather forecasting,
often face significant challenges related to explainability. They
frequently function as "black boxes" with limited transparency. Under-
standing complex weather dynamics is essential for accurate forecasting.
eXplainable AI (XAI) methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations
E n g i n e e r i n g A p p li c a t i o n s o f A r t i (cid:999) c i a l I n t e l l i g e n c e 162 (2025) 112335
(SHAP), Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), and
Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) help improve
transparency by clarifying how AI models generate their predictions
(Bhattacharya, 2022; Yang et al., 2023; Van Zyl et al., 2024). These
techniques enable meteorologists to identify patterns, evaluate corre-
lations, and assess the relative influence of input variables. The opaque
nature of many AI-based forecasting models raises concerns among
meteorologists about their reliability and trustworthiness. When it is
unclear how meteorological factors contribute to predictions or why
certain results are produced, it becomes difficult to evaluate outputs
critically or detect faulty correlations. XAI techniques, when used
alongside standard statistical evaluation methods, provide an opportu-
nity to demonstrate the reliability of AI-based weather models. By of-
fering logical and verifiable explanations, XAI allows experts to validate
model predictions against established meteorological knowledge, which
fosters greater confidence in AI-based forecasts.
7.1.3. Physical constraints
While many studies have demonstrated the advantages of AI tech-
niques over traditional modeling and statistical techniques in scientific
applications, concerns persist regarding the reliability and robustness of
the model, particularly in preserving fundamental physical principles. In
unfamiliar scenarios or outside the training domain, the model models
may deviate from established physical laws, which limits their gener-
alization capabilities. To address these challenges, Knowledge-Guided
Machine Learning (KGML), also known as Physics-Informed Machine
Learning (PIML), has emerged as a promising approach. This method
integrates physical laws and domain knowledge into ML or DL models to
enhance both accuracy and interpretability. For instance, Beucler et al.
(2019) showed that applying physical constraints improves the perfor-
mance and relevance of the model in climate modeling. Similarly,
studies by Wu et al. (2020) and Daw et al. (2020) demonstrated that
incorporating physics-based constraints allows more effective simula-
Earth’s
tion of complex systems such as turbulence and climate. This
results in models that exhibit greater consistency, improved generaliz-
ability, and better uncertainty estimation. One specific and increasingly
popular approach within PIML is the use of Physics-Informed NNs
(PINNs). These models embed physical laws, often in the form of Partial
Differential Equations (PDEs), directly into the loss functions of NNs. By
penalizing deviations from known physical behaviors during training,
PINNs ensure that the resulting models not only fit data but also respect
underlying scientific principles. This is particularly advantageous in
domains with limited or noisy data, where physical laws can serve as a
form of regularization and guidance. Integrating physical information
into AI-based forecasting models improves both prediction accuracy and
consistency with established physical relationships. This is especially
critical in meteorology, where variables such as temperature, pressure,
and humidity are closely interdependent. PIML methods, including
PINNs, enable the incorporation of these interactions into the modeling
process, which is essential for high-stakes applications like extreme
weather forecasting. Despite progress in AI-based weather forecasting,
challenges remain in modeling multiple meteorological parameters
simultaneously while capturing their nonlinear interactions. The highly
dynamic nature of the atmosphere means that variations in one
parameter can substantially influence others. Therefore, a more
comprehensive modeling strategy is necessary. The integration of PIML,
particularly PINNs, with conventional techniques offers a promising
pathway toward achieving physically consistent and scientifically
credible predictions.
7.1.4. Uncertainties and extreme weather prediction
Weather forecasts are inherently uncertain due to the complex and
chaotic nature of the atmosphere. This uncertainty requires predicting a
range of possible weather scenarios instead of relying on a single
deterministic forecast. Recent AI-based weather models have improved
forecast accuracy but mainly focus on deterministic predictions and

faster and more skillful than leading operational ensemble systems. This
method captures forecast uncertainty effectively and improves decision-
making in weather-sensitive fields. However, probabilistic approaches
like this are not yet widely adopted in other AI-based models. One
important benefit of ensemble-based weather forecasting is the oppor-
tunity to increase the accuracy of extreme weather predictions. Evalu-
ation of AI-based models in extreme weather prediction remains
incomplete. Despite progress in forecasting specific extremes such as
hail size (Billet et al., 1997), tornadoes (Adrianto et al., 2009), and
extreme precipitation (Radhika and Shashi, 2009; Herrmann and
Schumacher, 2018), and improvements in forecasting tropical cyclone
tracks and wind power production with GenCast, significant challenges
still persist. Meteorologists also identify prediction of out-of-distribution
severe weather events as a major problem for AI-based forecasting. In
addition, as mentioned earlier, the quality and resolution of input data,
especially initial conditions, are often insufficient for predicting
convective-scale processes, which are key to many extreme weather
events. Therefore, increasing the reliability and resolution of critical
parameters such as humidity in the initial conditions could significantly
enhance forecast skill.
7.1.5. Temporal adaptation and generalization
Given the dynamic nature of atmospheric systems, forecasting
models must continuously adapt to accurately capture changing weather
patterns. Without mechanisms to address temporal dynamics, models
risk becoming less effective, especially as environmental conditions
deviate from historical norms. Ongoing refinement is essential to ensure
precise and timely forecasts amid emerging climate trends. Moreover, AI
models often face limitations in generalizing to unseen or extreme
weather events beyond their original training data. This constraint re-
duces the robustness and reliability of such models, especially in critical
scenarios where accurate prediction is paramount. Addressing these
challenges by integrating temporal adaptation and enhancing general-
ization capabilities is crucial to advancing AI-based weather forecasting
and improving its practical utility. Previous research has insufficiently
addressed these critical aspects. To advance the field of weather fore-
casting, it is imperative that these oversights be rectified.
8. Conclusion
As discussed, AI-based weather forecasting has seen significant ad-
vancements in recent years, particularly since 2022, marking a mile-
stone in the field due to its remarkable flexibility, speed, and
computational efficiency. This paper presents a comprehensive review
of AI-based weather forecasting models, highlighting their rapid devel-
opment and key characteristics. It emphasizes the potential of these
models to improve forecasting accuracy. The evolution of AI methods
such as RNNs, CNNs, and GNNs, along with the development of models
like FourCastNet, GraphCast, GenCast, and more recent data-driven
models like Aardvark, demonstrates the growing capability of AI to
handle complex forecasting tasks and capture specific weather phe-
nomena. Considering the performance of the models and existing chal-
lenges in the field, moving forward, incorporating high-quality, high-
resolution data is essential to enhance the accuracy of small-scale
weather and extreme event predictions. While downscaling techniques
and autoregressive modeling approaches have contributed to progress,
further improvements in data, particularly humidity, will be critical for
refining forecast precision at convective scales. Additionally, enhancing
the explainability of AI models represents a vital direction for future
research. By integrating XAI techniques, transparency can be improved,
allowing meteorologists and researchers to better understand model
outputs, validate predictions, and identify potential sources of error.
Another promising avenue lies in embedding physical constraints into AI
models through PIML and PINNs. These approaches promote forecasts

frameworks like GenCast have demonstrated potential for more skillful
and reliable extreme weather forecasting, suggesting the need for wider
adoption and rigorous evaluation across diverse AI models.
