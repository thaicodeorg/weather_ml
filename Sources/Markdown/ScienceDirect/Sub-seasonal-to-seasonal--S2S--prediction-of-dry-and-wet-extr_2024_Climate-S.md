Extreme weather events, including heatwaves, cyclones, and floods,
are becoming more frequent in India due to climate change. Floods,

meteorological disasters. Predictions at the sub-seasonal to seasonal
(S2S) scale have recently acquired prominence due to enhanced

the future, surpassing recent records. In particular, the possibility of
droughts with up to a 30 % deficit in the seasonal mean rainfall is a
significant concern as droughts impact agriculture, water resources, and
overall economic growth of the country. The frequency of concurrent
hot and dry extremes is projected to increase, which is likely resulting in

Extreme weather forecasting is critical and reliable meteorological

Demirel et al., 2013; Fundel et al., 2013; Penning-Rowsell et al., 2000;

used for various purposes, including providing early warnings of
extreme events (drought, floods, cyclones, compound extremes) several
weeks in advance, assisting in disaster preparedness, improving agri-
cultural planning, and water resource management in India.
In recent years, the World Weather Research Program (WWRP) and
the World Climate Research Program (WCRP) launched the S2S pre-
diction project to improve sub-seasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) forecast skills
(Vitart and Robertson, 2018). The S2S project provides sub-seasonal to
seasonal forecasts from eleven operating centers to help in bridging
forecast and knowledge gaps in developing countries (Vitart et al.,
2017). The consequences of hydrologic extremes are often detrimental,
including substantial economic losses (Lucas et al., 2017). Therefore,
early warning systems at sub-seasonal to seasonal scales are needed for
climate change adaptation. The forecast skill in short to medium term

Moreover, the skill in the ensemble multi-model mean forecast of the

analysis.
Each model contains a control reforecast member that employs a
single unperturbed initial condition and perturbed forecast members
that account for uncertainty in the initial and boundary conditions. We
used the mean of all the ensemble members (control and perturbed) to
obtain a deterministic forecast that can be compared with the observed
data. In order to evaluate model performance, we collected the
maximum number of years of data that each model can provide during
the summer monsoon season (Table S1). For each month in the monsoon
season, we obtained data twice a month: once at the earliest initial date
possible and once in the middle of the month, which resulted in eight
samples for each model during the summer monsoon season. We pre-
pared observed data from IMD by matching the dates with each of the
S2S models to evaluate the forecast skill using different verification
metrics.

2.2. Evaluation of precipitation forecast
We obtained summer monsoon season precipitation from all nine S2S

correlation coefficient (r) to compare observations to the ensemble
mean of all reforecast members (control plus perturbed members) from a
forecast model. In addition, we used an additional score to evaluate and
rank each model in terms of their forecast skill, which includes different
attributes of verification to evaluate the overall performance of each
model. The overall performance score was used (Graham et al., 2019;

3.3. Forecast skill for dry and wet extremes in the Krishna river basin
After a severe drought in the summer monsoon season of 2009, the

districts in India witnessed drought-like conditions in 2009 (Ministry of
Agriculture, 2009). The drought of 2009 was one of the deadliest
droughts in India (Richard Mahapatra, 2001). Considering the temporal
variability of rainfall extremes during the summer monsoon, we exam-

the three better-performing S2S models (UKMO, ECC, and NCEP) and
the currently operational ERFS for the extended range forecast.

better forecast skill. To better determine spatial coverage and intensity
