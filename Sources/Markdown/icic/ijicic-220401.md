Abstract.
In recent years, the importance of quantitative precipitation forecasting has
increased in flood control and water resource management. This study aimed to improve
the accuracy of quantitative precipitation forecasting. The proposed method is a hybrid
deep learning model that combines a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network with a
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). Although conventional LSTM models are effective
for learning temporal patterns, they cannot capture the spatial relationships among ob-
servation stations. In contrast, the proposed model introduces spatial features by utilizing
CNN to learn the location information of observation points. The contributions of this
study are as follows. First, spatial information is incorporated using CNN, enabling the
model to capture spatial dependencies among observation stations. Second, experiments
using observational data from 13 stations in Gunma Prefecture confirmed that the model
improved 1-hour-ahead quantitative precipitation forecasting accuracy. Third, the evalua-
tion across multiple forecast lead times (1-6 h) shows that the proposed method performs
best for 1-hour-ahead predictions. The CNN+LSTM hybrid model achieved better perfor-
mance on average than the standalone LSTM model in terms of Root Mean Square Error
(RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Threat Score (TS), particularly for stations
surrounded by densely located neighbors. These results suggest that the introduction of
spatial information is effective in improving the accuracy of short-term quantitative pre-
cipitation forecasting.
Keywords: Precipitation, Rainfall prediction, LSTM, CNN, Deep learning, Machine
learning
1. Introduction. Quantitative precipitation forecasting is important for preventing
floods and landslides from occurring. Quantitative precipitation forecasting is also used
for water resource utilization. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have incor-
porated precipitation forecasting into specific cases [1, 2]. Sikder et al. have proposed a
technique that rapidly updates flood forecast maps using quantitative precipitation fore-
casting [1]. Ceppi et al. have used quantitative precipitation forecasting for irrigation [2].
This helps manage the amount of water used.

Data assimilation is a major method for quantitative precipitation forecasting. Miyoshi
et al. have performed a 30-minute advance prediction [3]. They have used satellite obser-
vation data with a data assimilation approach. Kita et al. have reported an improvement
in precipitation forecast accuracy by assimilating X-band MP radar data using a three-
dimensional variational method [4]. Improving quantitative precipitation forecasting using
data assimilation and numerical models is still important. However, this method requires
spatiotemporally abundant observational data from satellites and radars. Therefore, it
is difficult to apply this approach to quantitative precipitation forecasting in nonurban
areas. Alternative quantitative precipitation forecasting methods are important.
Deep learning has been applied in many fields [5]. This trend has also been observed
in weather forecasting [6]. Research in this area is advancing steadily. Matsuoka et al.
have used deep learning to predict cloud clusters [6]. They have forecasted cumulonimbus
clouds before the formation of tropical cyclones. Suzuki et al. have applied a Convolution-
al Neural Network (CNN) to predicting heavy rainfall [7]. Deep learning has also been
applied to spatiotemporal forecasting problems beyond meteorology. For example, taxi
demand prediction has been studied using spatiotemporal graph attention networks [8].
In addition, seq2seq models have been used to generate continuous multi-step forecasts
for hydrological time-series predictions. For example, Xiang et al. have applied an LSTM-
based seq2seq model to hourly rainfall-runoff forecasting and evaluated it using bias and
RMSE-related metrics [9].
Deep learning methods have also been applied to quantitative precipitation forecasting
[10]. Recent studies have increasingly focused on spatiotemporal deep learning models.
Radar echo maps have been used for short-term rainfall prediction with spatiotemporal
recurrent networks enhanced by attention mechanisms [11]. Beyond image-based inputs,
recent studies have proposed models that explicitly represent the complex spatial interac-
tions. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been introduced to learn spatial dependencies
and couple multiple physical factors for precipitation forecasting [12]. In addition, station-
graph-based Spatiotemporal Graph Neural Networks (ST-GNNs) have been proposed for
rainfall forecasting using meteorological station networks [13]. Transformer-based archi-
tectures have also been applied to rainfall prediction using observation-derived atmo-
spheric information such as GNSS Precipitable Water Vapor (PWV) [14]. These studies
indicate that explicitly incorporating spatial information is important for improving the
precipitation forecasting performance.
Kaneko and Nakayoshi have proposed a method to predict precipitation one hour lat-
er in Kyushu, Japan, by learning data from 114 sites in Kyushu, Japan. This method
adapts the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) [15]. This architecture is specialized for
learning time-series data. This study has demonstrated the potential of the LSTM model
for precipitation prediction. However, this poses a challenge. LSTM-based quantitative
precipitation forecasting cannot learn the spatial locations of the observation stations.
Therefore, it may fail to capture the spatial relationships between the stations. If the
model learns both the time series and station locations, the prediction accuracy may
improve.
This study aims to improve the accuracy of quantitative precipitation forecasting us-
ing LSTM. However, a key limitation of LSTM is its inability to consider the spatial
locations of the observation stations. To address this issue, we propose a method that
combines LSTM with CNN. A CNN is used to extract the coordinate data from each
station. This spatial information helps the LSTM to learn the station locations. In the
proposed method, each observation station is mapped to a fixed grid based on its latitude
and longitude. A CNN extracts spatial features from the grid-based representation. These
features are then fed into LSTM layers for temporal modeling. As a result, the model can

2. Data. Data are obtained from the Japan Meteorological Agency website [16] for 13
observation stations in Gunma Prefecture, Japan. The Japan Meteorological Agency pro-
vides observational data for three types of stations. The first type is a four-element sta-
tion. These stations measure the temperature, precipitation, wind speed, and sunshine
duration. The second type is rainfall stations that measure precipitation. These stations
record only precipitation. The third type is meteorological station. It observes temper-
ature, precipitation, wind speed, sunshine duration, pressure, and relative humidity. In
this study, we limit the number of stations from which to obtain data to “meteorological
station” and “four elements stations” in Gunma Prefecture, as shown in Figure 1 [17, 18].
Data are obtained from 13 stations. Hourly data from each observation station are used
for training. We use data from January 1, 1996, 1:00 AM to January 1, 2022, 0:00 AM
for training. In addition, data from 1:00 AM on January 1, 2022, to 0:00 AM on January
16, 2025, are used as test data.

3. Model. In this case, we use the LSTM model, which predicts precipitation for the next
hour based on 12 h of observational data. Each dataset contains input from the previous
12 h at each station. The target output is a quantitative precipitation forecasting for one
hour later. The model is evaluated for one-hour-ahead forecasts as well as for 2, 3, 4, 5,
and 6 h ahead. In each case, the model uses observational data from the previous 12 h as
input. The target output is set to the corresponding future time.
Because there are missing measurements in the observation data, the values are inter-
polated using linear interpolation according to the number of missing measurements. As
proposed in [15], the screening procedure is as follows.
1) If there are missing temperature data, linear interpolation is performed if the missing
data are within 4 hours in a row.
2) If precipitation and wind speed are absent, linear interpolation is performed if the
absence is within 1 hour continuously.
For missing value processing, we adopt the same method as Kaneko and Nakayoshi [15].
Linear interpolation is applied only to short gaps in the observation records. Specifically,
temperature data are interpolated when the missing period is within four consecutive
hours, whereas precipitation and wind data are interpolated only when the missing period
is within one hour. A one-hour gap is the minimum possible gap in our hourly records, and
restricting interpolation to this case reduces the risk of creating spurious rainfall events.
Some missing values may remain even after the screening. If any of the observed data for
each feature for the past 12 h and the precipitation data for one hour later to be forecast
contain missing measurements, that dataset is not used.

We use Keras (3.8.0) and TensorFlow (2.18.0) to build the LSTM model. The model
has three LSTM layers. These are combined and output as the final output. The first and
second LSTM layers output time-series representations, and the final LSTM layer outputs
a fixed-length representation for the precipitation prediction. A dropout is applied after
each LSTM layer to reducing overfitting.
We describe a CNN that encodes the spatial arrangement of the stations. Each ob-
servation station is placed on a fixed grid based on its latitude and longitude. We first
determine the latitude and longitude ranges from the station coordinates. We then draw
grid lines that are evenly spaced within these ranges. Each station is assigned to the
nearest grid cell. If a grid cell contains no station, the feature values of that cell are set to
zero. This grid representation allows the model to use station location information. Each
grid cell (row, col) stores the time series of the features for the station assigned to that
cell.
The data pass through a CNN and are flattened into a 1D vector. This vector is input
into the first LSTM layer. The setup of the CNN is described as follows. First, a 2D
convolution layer (Conv2D) is applied at each time step to extracting spatial features. The
ReLU function is used as the activation function to perform nonlinear transformations.
By setting the padding to SAME, convolution is performed while maintaining the input
size and edge information. Next, a pooling layer (MaxPooling2D) is applied. This layer
extracts important features while reducing the size of the input data. In the final step,

5. Results. In the experiments, we compare our forecasts with two simple baseline meth-
ods: persistence and moving-average methods. In the persistence method, the predicted
precipitation at the future time step is set to be equal to the most recent observed pre-
cipitation. This method is equivalent to the naive forecasting method [19]. In the moving-
average method, the prediction is calculated as the average precipitation over the past k
hours, where k denotes the window length [19]. As an additional reference model, we also
include a Spatiotemporal Graph Neural Network (ST-GNN), which explicitly models the
spatial dependencies among observation stations [13]. In this method, each station is treat-
ed as a node in the graph. Edges are constructed using a k-nearest-neighbor graph based
on geographical coordinates. At each time step, the node features consist of temperature,
wind, and precipitation. Spatial features are extracted using a Graph Convolutional Net-
work (GCN), followed by a residual GCN block and a Graph Attention Network (GAT)
layer. The target station embeddings over the previous 12 h are then fed into a bidirection-
al Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) to model the temporal dependencies. A fully connected

The spatial grid resolution has been set to six rows and six columns. The grid interval
has been approximately 0.15 degrees in latitude and 0.21 degrees in longitude. In this grid
mapping, no multiple observation stations have been assigned to the same grid cell. This
choice balances the spatial resolution and computational cost, given the limited number of
observation stations. The input sequence length has been set to 12 h. We have evaluated
forecast lead times from 1 to 6 h ahead to examine how the accuracy changes with
longer horizons. For the CNN component, we have used 32 filters to extract the spatial
features from the grid-based representation. This setting has maintained a lightweight
model and has helped avoid GPU memory issues. In preliminary trials, increasing the
number of filters substantially has increased the computational cost and has made the
training unstable, whereas the resulting accuracy gain has been limited. For temporal
modeling, three LSTM layers have been set to 512, 256, and 256 units. This configuration
has balanced the model capacity and generalization performance. Smaller hidden sizes
have reduced forecasting accuracy. In contrast, larger hidden sizes have tended to cause
unstable training and overfitting issues. The dropout rates have been set to 20% and 50%
for the first and second and third LSTM layers, respectively. A dropout has been applied
after each LSTM layer to suppressing overfitting. Higher dropout rates have been used in
the deeper layers, where overfitting has been more likely to occur. The first and second
LSTM layers have used a many-to-many setting and output time-series representations.
The third LSTM layer has used a many-to-one setting and output a fixed-length vector.
We have used the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001. The loss function has
been defined as the mean squared error. The batch size has been set to 128 samples. The
model has been trained for 50 epochs using the Adam optimizer. For TS, the threshold
value of precipitation has been set to a = 1 [mm/h].

6. Conclusions. In this study, we propose a CNN+LSTM hybrid model for short-term
quantitative precipitation forecasting using observations from 13 stations in Gunma Pre-
fecture. The main contribution of this study is to incorporate station location information
through a CNN-based spatial representation and combine it with temporal modeling us-
ing LSTM. In our experiments, the proposed model achieved better performance than the
standalone LSTM model and simple baseline methods for stations surrounded by dense-
ly located neighbors. These results suggest that explicitly introducing spatial context is
beneficial when sufficient neighboring observations are available. However, the average
improvements over the standalone LSTM model were modest, and we did not conduct
statistical significance testing. Therefore, our current results should be interpreted as ev-
idence of their potential usefulness. From a practical viewpoint, the observed dependence
on station density indicates a limitation in the current spatial representation. The benefit
of spatial modeling may diminish in regions with sparse stations. This finding suggests

an important scientific implication: the value of spatial information in station-based fore-
casting depends strongly on the availability and arrangement of neighboring observations.
In this paper, we demonstrate that the proposed CNN+LSTM approach can improve
short-term precipitation forecasting. However, it is necessary to further examine whether
these observed improvements are statistically robust. Moreover, the comparative study
should be expanded to include other spatiotemporal architectures such as attention-based
and transformer-based models. Further improvement of the spatial representation is re-
quired by incorporating additional geographic information such as elevation. In addition,
spatial coverage should be enhanced using additional observation points or auxiliary data
sources. These problems will be addressed in future studies to clarify the conditions under
which the proposed CNN+LSTM approach provides meaningful gains and to improve the
robustness and generalization performance of short-term precipitation forecasting.
