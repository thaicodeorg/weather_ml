l
a
19 The rapid convergence of physics-based mondeling and data-driven artificial intelligence (AI) has revolutionized the approach
20 of scientists to complex problems in Earth system science (Waqas et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024b). Over the past decade,
r
21 machine learning (ML) and deep learuning (DL) have been used to detect nonlinear patterns and spatiotemporal associations in
22 large environmental datasets, which can be used to improve the predictive accuracy of weather forecasting and hydrological
o
23 modeling (Mathew et al., 2021; Waqas et al., 2023). However, conventional ML models, despite their flexibility and
24 computational efficiency, oftJen disregard physical constraints, resulting in poor generalization, physical inconsistencies, and
25 reduced reliability in unobserved environmental conditions (Barbierato & Gatti, 2024; Zaker Esteghamati et al., 2025). Physics-
26 informed neural networks (PINNs) have been proposed to fill this gap by integrating physical systems (typically represented
27 as ordinary or partial differential equations (ODEs/PDEs)) into neural network training (Habib et al., 2025; Lawal et al., 2022).
28 PINNs embed physics in the loss function, integrating data-driven learning and first-principles modeling to ensure accurate and
29 physically consistent predictions, even in regimes of data scarcity or noise (Haitsiukevich, 2025).
30 In weather and hydrological sciences, non-linear dynamics, including atmospheric convection, precipitation, evapotranspiration,
31 and runoff generation, are best modeled by integrating complex multi-scale processes that are solved using the Navier-Stokes,
32 energy balance, and mass conservation equations (Kim, 2013; Sivapalan et al., 2001; Yang et al., 2021). Numerical weather
33 prediction (NWP) and hydrological simulation models are computationally intensive and sensitive to errors in parameterization
34 and boundary conditions, as well as data assimilation uncertainties (Kashinath et al., 2021; Schultz et al., 2021). Conversely,
35 ML/DL models can quickly mimic such processes but tend to overfit observational data, often failing to respect conservation
36 laws (Dhanka et al., 2025). With the introduction of PINNs, a middle ground is offered that leverages both paradigms, imposing
37 physical constraints on the learning process to increase interpretability, stability, and efficiency (Cai et al., 2021; Lawal et al.,
38 2022). The use of PINNs in the atmospheric and hydrological setup, therefore, represents an essential methodological
39 development that can overcome the drawbacks of exclusively physical and exclusively data-driven models (Zeinali et al., 2025).
1

40 PINNs have been highly successful in numerous geoscientific disciplines (Zhao et al., 2024b). More specifically, in
41 groundwater and flood modeling, PINNs have been used to estimate solutions to the Shallow Water Equations and have been
42 shown to scale well for reconstructing flow fields using sparse or incomplete observations, while remaining physically faithful
43 (Donnelly et al., 2024; Zhang, 2024). They have been used in atmospheric and climate modeling to simulate the dynamics of
44 turbulent flows, parameterize subgrid processes, and downscale climate projections, while remaining energy- and momentum-
45 conserving (Zhao et al., 2024b). The PINN framework has also been applied to related areas such as power systems (Huang &
46 Wang, 2022), geotechnical engineering (Yuan et al., 2025), and aerospace simulation (Tkachov & Murashko, 2025),
47 demonstrating its high versatility. Although these methods have been used in engineering and physical sciences, their systematic
48 use and assessment in weather and hydrological modeling remain scattered and understudied (Waqas et al., 2024).
49 Existing reviews of physics-informed ML (PIML) and PINNs have been more subjective than existing reviews, focusing on a
50 more general geoscientific or engineering perspective. Habib et al. (2025) provided a broad overview of the applicability of
51 PINNs in geosciences, including hydrology, seismology, and atmospheric sciences. They mentioned that these could be used
f
52 to improve computational efficiency and predictive accuracy (Habib et al., 2025)o. Similarly, Chew et al. (2025) studied the
53 applications of PIML in the design of urban infrastructure and the development of resilience, noting that integrating physical
o
54 constraints into ML models can enhance generalization and reduce the need for extensive sensor networks (Chew et al., 2025).
r
55 Yuan et al. (2025) found the interpretability benefits of PIML in the geotechnical context (Yuan et al., 2025), but Huang and
p
56 Wang (2022) identified several paradigms of PIML, such as physics-guided (Huang & Wang, 2022). However, none of these
57 reviews have addressed the unique issues and methodological challe-nges of weather and hydrological systems, where multi-
e
58 scale coupling, stochastic fluctuations, and non-stationarity are prevalent phenomena. Thus, a dedicated synthesis should be
59 conducted to examine the advances made by PINNs in construrcting hydrological and atmospheric models.
P
60 This review was designed in response to the growing recognition that traditional hydrological and meteorological models
61 struggle to balance physical rigor with data adaptabililty under varying climatic conditions. Conventional PIMLs, such as SWOT,
a
62 VIC, and WRF, necessitate considerable calibration and are highly computationally intensive, whereas data-driven models are
63 not robust to extrapolation (Eder, 2024; Schunltz et al., 2021; Waqas et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025).
r
64 Despite their limitations, PINNs faciulitate the implementation of hydrological and atmospheric governing equations within
65 neural network structures (Ali et al., 2024). For example, the solution of the one-dimensional Saint-Venant equation or the two-
o
66 dimensional Shallow Water Equation using PINNs has resulted in significant computational cost savings compared to numerical
J
67 solvers and in physically realistic predictions of flood wave propagation and river discharge (Feng et al., 2023). PIML has also
68 been applied in atmospheric sciences to simulate convection-resolving models, improving the temporal stability and physical
69 understanding of learned patterns (Beucler et al., 2023; Mukkavilli et al., 2023). Although progress has been made, challenges
70 persist, particularly in terms of training stability, scaling to high-dimensional problems, and compatibility with existing Earth
71 system models. These restrictions highlight the need for a systematic analysis of existing practices and the determination of
72 future research directions.
73 The current developments in PIML represent a paradigm shift in computational modeling, focusing on the interplay between
74 domain knowledge and data-driven inference (Tkachov & Murashko, 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). PIML and PINN frameworks
75 are capable of the previously mentioned computational intractability because of their ability to combine the interpretability of
76 physics-based formulations with the flexibility of neural networks (Farias et al., 2025; Huynh, 2023). In weather prediction,
77 for example, short-term rainfall prediction, parameter calibration in land-atmosphere interaction models, and bias correction in
78 NWP output, PINNs have been investigated. They have been used in hydrology to study groundwater flow, sediment transport,
79 infiltration, and flood forecasting (Habib et al., 2025; Zeinali et al., 2025). These applications demonstrate the flexibility of
80 PINNs; however, they also highlight critical gaps in research, including the lack of field-scale validation experiments,
81 quantification of uncertainty, and challenges in modeling interacting surface and subsurface processes.
82 Recent reviews in related fields can help overcome these challenges. Cai et al. (2021) surveyed the applications of PINNs to
83 fluid mechanics, with a focus on inverse problems in which the boundary conditions or material properties are unknown, a
2

84 situation typical of atmospheric and hydrological systems (Cai et al., 2021). Similarly, Yu et al. (2025) followed the history of
85 AI-based remote sensing inversion development, starting with physics-based models and moving to foundation models, and
86 found that several issues remain, including interpretability, domain generalization, and uncertainty quantification (Yu et al.,
87 2025). The findings can be integrated into the hydrometeorological setting to guide the construction of PINNs capable of
88 processing high-dimensional spatiotemporal data, mixed observations and models, and complicated boundary interactions. In
89 addition, the recent rise in the popularity of digital twins and hybrid physics-AI systems in Earth system surveillance (Chew et
90 al., 2025) warrants further consideration. PINNs are poised to play a central role in enabling continuous, physically consistent
91 data assimilation and forecast refinement (Chew et al., 2025).
92 Another critical motivation for this review is the lack of a unified taxonomy for evaluating PINN architectures in environmental
93 applications. While structured frameworks have been developed for other fields—for example, the four-tier taxonomy proposed
94 for aerospace engineering, weather, and hydrology–they lack comparable classification schemes (Tkachov & Murashko, 2025).
f
95 An existing gap in this area hinders systematic comparative analysis across studieos and the identification of optimal practices
96 for model design, training, and validation. Moreover, the literature on PINNs used alongside operational forecasting systems
o
97 or ensemble modeling frameworks is also sparse. However, it goes without saying that physics-informed surrogates have the
98 potential to reduce computational expenses in ensemble-based data assimilartion and uncertainty propagation. As such, current
p
99 synthesis methods and a consistent research agenda are needed to transform PINNs from experimental prototypes into
100 operational hydrometeorological systems. -

170 3.1. Overview of PINNs
171 PINNs are a set of DL models that directly incorporate physical laws (typically expressed as PDEs) into the neural network
172 training objective (Huang & Wang, 2022; Raissi et al., 2019). The architecture of PINNs and the training process are illustrated
173 in Fig. 2. (a) The conceptual framework combines neural network outputs with physical knowledge by incorporating PDEs and
174 relevant physical conditions. (b) The network is optimized during training by minimizing a composite loss that combines data-
175 constrained and physics-based residuals, thereby improving the model's consistency with governing hydrological and
176 atmospheric processes. It improves the model's generalization to weather and hydrological predictions (Bandai & Ghezzehei,
f
177 2022; Feng et al., 2023).

587 3.4. Difference between PINNs and CNNs
n
588 It is not necessary to view PINNs and CNNs as competing model classes. CNNs are designed for gridded data, while PINNs
r
589 are designed by training and regularization principles, where physical equations, constraints, or priors are added to the loss
u
590 function, architecture, or parameterization. Physics-informed CNNs, convolutional surrogates with PDE residual losses, and
o
591 vision models with physical priors are thus valid hybrids. If dense gridded observations are the dominant feature of the problem,
592 or if reanalysis products areJ used, CNNs are often a better choice, whereas if physical constraints, sparse labels, inverse
593 estimation, extrapolation beyond the training data, or conservation laws are more relevant, PINNs are more appealing.
594 PINNs and CNNs are distinct concepts within a DL framework for environmental modeling (Jia et al., 2025). Although they
595 can be classified as part of the broader subcategory of neural architecture, they are distinguished by significant differences in
596 design, training objectives, and mathematical concepts (Cerniauskas et al., 2024; Minadakis, 2024). CNNs are more data-driven
597 learning models, which are based on statistical learning on a large dataset (Waqas et al., 2024; Waqas et al., 2023), unlike
598 PINNs that explicitly include physical laws in the form of PDEs in the learning process (Cai et al., 2021; DUTTA & DAS,
599 2025; Jagtap & Karniadakis, 2020; Kharazmi et al., 2021; Pang et al., 2020). These differences are essential for weather, climate,
600 and hydrological modeling.
601 CNNs were initially developed to operate on grid-based spatial data by exploiting local spatial correlations using convolutional
602 filters and weight sharing (Yu & Ji, 2019). This design also enforces translation equivariance, allowing for the efficient
603 detection of features (edges, textures, or spatial gradients) in an image or field (LeCun et al., 2015; Yu & Ji, 2019).
604 CNNs have proven helpful in geophysical modeling for forecasting precipitation, creating subsurface satellite-based maps, and
605 anticipating hydrological conditions, with support from exceedingly large datasets (Liu et al., 2020; Shahriar et al., 2025; Yu
606 et al., 2022). The network is trained to learn a mapping 𝑓 : 𝑋 → 𝑌. However, CNNs do not assume the physics of the system;
0
607 therefore, they only generalize to the space of the training data (Tsourounis et al., 2022).
21

608 In comparison, a PINN integrates the guiding PDEs into the training problem, transforming the regression problem into a
609 physics-regularized optimization problem (Huang & Wang, 2022; Raissi et al., 2019). PINN networks are not only trained on
610 data but also minimize a composite loss that includes data misfit, boundary, and initial condition errors, as well as residuals
611 from the governing PDE (Farea et al., 2024; Khalid et al., 2024). The spatial and temporal derivatives can also be correctly
612 calculated with respect to the inputs using automatic differentiation, ensuring that the solution obtained is consistent with the
613 conservation laws and physical constraints (Wang & Yang, 2024). PINNs can effectively extrapolate to thus far unmonitored
614 spatiotemporal areas, which is one of the principal advantages of hydrological and meteorological prognostication; the
615 information is often incomplete or unevenly distributed (Farea et al., 2024; Khalid et al., 2024).
616 There operate at various architectural, structural, and computational levels. CNNs are discrete, grid-based, and hierarchical,
617 and use local receptive fields and convolutional kernels to extract spatial patterns (Malik et al., 2023). In contrast, PINNs are
618 usually continuous, mesh-free, and founded on fully connected MLPs, which are expected to model continuous functions with
619 constant domains (Zhao et al., 2024a). The implications of these architectural differences extend well beyond representational
f
620 strength, training behavior and computational efficiency. o

644 The rapid evolution of PINNs has transformed the way environmental scientists approach nonlinear and multi-scale problems
o

648 4.1. Dominant modeling architectures used for PINNs ande their variants
649 This study highlighted current PINN architectures, includinrg vanilla PINNs, vPINNs, XPINNs, cPINNs, and sPINNs, all
P
650 optimized for particular categories of physical and computational problems (Hu et al., 2021; Jagtap & Karniadakis, 2020;
651 Kharazmi et al., 2021; Nazari, 2025; Raissi et al., 2019; Shukla et al., 2021). Vanilla PINNs are basic yet versatile and user-
652 friendly PINNs that utilize an MLP to reduce a colmposite loss, incorporating data, boundary, and physical residual terms
a
653 (Bandai & Ghezzehei, 2022; Feng et al., 2023). However, it tends to cause convergence instability in stiff or multi-scale
n
654 problems when enforcing PDE residuals.
r
655 To address these issues, vPINNs refourmulate the loss in the weak (integral) form, thereby simplifying numerical conditioning
656 and the handling of discontinuities in hydraulic head or flux within heterogeneous hydrological systems (Jia et al., 2025;
o
657 Kharazmi et al., 2021; Kharazmi et al., 2019). Similarly, XPINNs develop this concept using domain decomposition, parallel
658 training of subdomains, and Jinterface continuity (Jagtap & Karniadakis, 2020). Specifically, this decomposition enables the
659 modeling of extremely heterogeneous systems, such as stratified aquifers or heterogeneous layers with different stability in the
660 atmosphere, in a manner that conserves the conservation laws at the interface boundaries, which is an essential consideration
661 in the surface-subsurface hydrological interaction of the atmosphere and the surface (Alhubail et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2021;
662 Secci et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024).
663 The uncertainty in sPINNs injects random variables into the network, which can be tracked within a physics-based framework
664 (Chen et al., 2021; Habib et al., 2025). With these architectures, the impact of uncertain inputs, such as variable rainfall, soil
665 heterogeneity, and boundary fluxes, is measured to provide physically consistent estimates of uncertainty. Fractional and mesh-
666 free PINNs go further, with the variants of the models being capable of modeling memory and nonlocal transport processes
667 (Ma et al., 2025; Pang et al., 2019), and mesh-free models not necessitating a grid to be generated in irregular regions, such as
668 floodplains and changing watersheds (Zeinali et al., 2025).
669 Overall, these structural developments suggest a hybridization tendency: spatial adaptivity, stochastic representation, and
670 domain decomposition (Ji et al., 2024). Hybrid PINNs are also taking over as surrogate solvers for numerical solvers, achieving
671 the same level of accuracy at a much lower computational cost (Donnelly et al., 2024; Nazari, 2025; Nazari et al., 2022).
672 The range of these architectures reflects the flexibility of the PINN paradigm in managing the equations that govern
673 environmental systems, spanning Navier-Stokes-based atmospheric models to the Richards equation in unsaturated hydrology.
24

674 4.2. Reconciling data-driven learning and physical constraints
675 The reviewed literature suggests that PINNs can be successfully used in the data-driven learning/physical constraints procedure
676 to develop composite loss functions that reconcile the requirements of empirical and theoretical consistency (Farea et al., 2024).
677 This two-sidedness enables PINNs to act as physics-regularized regressors, ensuring robust predictions that can be interpreted
678 even when the observational data are sparse or noisy (Habib et al., 2025).
679 The challenge of data scarcity and its heterogeneity is a classical issue in hydrology, whereas governing equations, such as the
680 Darcy law, Saint-Venant system, or Richards equation, can be used to regularize learning with PINNs (Bandai & Ghezzehei,
681 2022; Feng et al., 2023). PINNs have been used across the atmospheric sciences, where nonlinearity and chaos are expected,
682 and have been applied to the computation of convection-resolving and downscale coarse-resolution forecasts without mass or
683 energy conservation (Beucler et al., 2023). The indication of such applications suggests that physics-directed priority can be
684 combined with sparse sensor measurements or model outputs using PINNs, resulting in superior generalization and reduced
f
685 uncertainty. o
o
686 However, the trade-off between the database and physics-based term of the loss is a technical problem. Overweighting of
687 physics residuals can impede learning in noisy domains, whereas underweigrhting can yield physically erratic answers (Braga-
p
688 Neto, 2021; McClenny & Braga-Neto, 2023). Adaptive weighting strategies, self-attention PINNs, and vPINNs have been
689 proposed as solutions to this imbalance by dynamically adjusting the- role of each loss term during training (Braga-Neto, 2021;
e
690 Kharazmi et al., 2021). PINNs can be used with data assimilation models, such as ensemble Kalman filters and four-dimensional
691 variational models, to combine real-time measurements with prhysics-constrained learning to produce improved forecasts (Hu
692 et al., 2025; Kashinath et al., 2021). P
693 4.3. Limitations and prospects of implementation of PINNs/variants
l
a
694 In terms of operational forecasting, PINNs nare best suited as hybrid rather than stand-alone elements of numerical weather
695 prediction or hydrological models. The practical applications involve (i) using PINNs as an expensive subgrid process surrogate;
r
696 (ii) coupling PINNs with ensemble Kalman filters, 3D-Var, or 4D-Var to assimilate sparse station, radar, satellite, and
u
697 reanalysis data; (iii) embedding PINN modules within the hydrological digital twins for near real-time state estimation; and (iv)
o
698 using PINNs to reconstruct missing fields and poorly observed parameters, such as hydraulic conductivity, roughness,
699 infiltration capacity, and bouJndary fluxes.
700 The deployment restrictions are still very high. Automatic differentiation can be slower than traditional neural networks because
701 it requires repeating the evaluation of PDE residuals at numerous collocation points. The availability of data is variable over
702 basins and events, boundaries are frequently unknown, and operational systems must have strict latency, reliability, calibration,
703 and uncertainty communication requirements. For this reason, PINNs are best considered decision-support and model-
704 augmentation tools at this time, unless they have been compared to operational observations and local numerical solvers within
705 the target lead time and spatial resolution.
706 While promising, PINNs have challenges associated with the technique as well as hydrometeorological deployment. The
707 technique-level limitations comprise training instability for stiff and multi-scale PDEs, spectral bias, gradient imbalance
708 between data and physics losses, sensitivity to collocation-point sampling, hard boundary conditions, and computational cost
709 due to automatic differentiation. The hydrometeorological limitations are due to uncertainties in forcing and boundary
710 conditions, noisy and sparse field observations, parameter equifinality, spatial variability, and lack of validation in high-
711 dimensional forecasts at basin, regional, or continental scales.
712 PINNs are also susceptible to convergence instability methodologically in stiff and multi-scale systems, whose scales of the
713 loss components are of different magnitudes (Wang & Yang, 2024). Systems that are ill-posed in inverse problems, where the
714 parameters of the system being learned are determined along with the state variables, can have multiple minima that represent
25

715 physically distinct solutions (Depina et al., 2022). Another frontier actively pursued is uncertainty quantification, which is
716 addressed through stochastic extensions (sPINNs) and Bayesian PINNs that capture probabilistic parameter distributions (Ji et
717 al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025).
718 There is a need to qualify performance claims. There are several potential advantages of PINNs, including a decreased amount
719 of labelled data needed for training or reduced training time after training; however, computational advantages are problem-
720 dependent and should be backed up by quantifiable metrics such as wall clock training time, inference time, memory usage,
721 conservation residuals, RMSE/MAE/NSE, or a comparison with calibrated numerical solvers and purely data-driven baselines.
722 Algorithmic gains rely heavily on the physical problem, data regime, and optimization approach, as highlighted by related
723 recent work for robust PINNs and intelligent optimization of thermal/fluid systems (Abdulkarim et al., 2026; Demir et al., 2025;
724 Nooruldeen et al., 2025), and the significance of operational data representation and climate-relevant decision contexts is
725 highlighted in wider studies of climate and energy systems (Salman et al., 2021; Salman et al., 2025).
f
726 Although the potential of PINNs in weather and hydrological modeling is apparoent, several technical, methodological, and
727 operational limitations exist. The most important limitations are associated woith computational cost, convergence instability,
728 data sparsity, and scalability, whereas the opportunities are hybrid algorithmic integration, uncertainty quantification, and
r

731 5. Conclusion
732 This review highlights the transformative potential of PINNs in bridging the gap between data-driven learning and first-
733 principles modeling in weather and hydrological modeling. PINNs provide a unified framework for learning non-linear and
734 multi-scale processes, achieved through a single neural net that incorporates governing equations, thereby ensuring physical
735 consistency. The vPINNs, XPINNs, cPINNs, sPINNs, fPINNs, and mesh-free PINNs address domain-related problems,
736 including discontinuities, stochastic variability, anomalous diffusion, and irregular geometries. The combination of all these
737 architectures introduces the flexibility of PINNs and makes them stronger when dealing with complex environmental systems.
738 Studies have demonstrated the application of PINNs in modeling the dynamics of convection-driven atmospheric flows,
739 propagating flood waves, estimating groundwater properties, and synthesizing sparse or uncertain data. It is also worth noting
740 that PINNs are not only surrogates for computationally expensive numerical solvers but also interpretable models that obey
741 conservation laws. However, certain limitations are also evident, as seen in the review. PINNs require computationally intensive
f
742 training, are unstable owing to stiff or high-dimensional problems, are sensitive to nooise, and lack standardized frameworks for
743 comparing results in hydrometeorological problems.
o
r
744 Recent developments, including adaptive sampling, neural operators, and hybrid systems that integrate PINNs with ensemble-
p
745 based prediction and foundation models, have the potential to be scalable and viable solutions. Digital twins also consider
-
746 PINNs a significant component of real-time observation of Earth systems, where precision, physical fidelity, and computational
e
747 efficiency are essential.
r
P
748 PINNs can be considered as a family of models that are regularized by physics, and their usefulness varies based on the problem
749 structure. They are suitable if the equations governing the system are reliable, observations are few, the parameters to be
750 inverted are significant, or conservation constraints mlust be satisfied. They are less appealing in the case of dense labeled data,
a
751 when physical constraints are less well understood, or when operational latency and robustness take precedence over physical
n
752 interpretability. Reproducible benchmarking, field-scale validation, uncertainty quantification, and integration with current
753 forecasting and data-assimilation infrastrurcture will become key to future progress.
u
754 Future research should focus on scalable architectures, the integration of PINN with numerical models, and uncertainty
o
755 quantification methods to enhance reliability. The suggested improvements to hybrid frameworks, foundation models, and
J
756 adaptive training strategies aim to develop physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) for real-time hydrometeorological
757 forecasting.
