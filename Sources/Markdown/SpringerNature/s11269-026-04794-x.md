Abstract
This study investigates the efficacy of Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) models
for forecasting seasonal climatic variables such as rainfall, maximum temperature, and
minimum temperature. The data were sourced from the World Bank Climate Change
Knowledge Portal covering the period from January 1901 to December 2022, represent-
ing monthly climate records for India. Using this century-long dataset, the study compares
Bayesian methods (BSTS and BSTSX) with traditional statistical methods (SARIMA,
SARIMAX) and advanced machine learning approaches (XGBoost, LSTM). Lower values
of root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percent-
age error (MAPE), and mean absolute scaled error (MASE) were used to evaluate each
model. The study reveals that Bayesian models outperform competing methods in terms
of accuracy and interpretability for all three seasonal time series. The Bayesian methods
improved forecast accuracy by approximately 59–66% for the rainfall series, 90–93%
for maximum temperature, and 40–45% for minimum temperature, relative to competing
models, based on MAPE computed from the testing set. It is acknowledged that SARI-
MA achieved marginally lower testing-set RMSE (0.516) and MAPE (2.230) than BSTS
(RMSE: 0.614; MAPE: 2.935) for the minimum temperature series. The results show that
the Bayesian structural framework is a reliable tool for climate-sensitive applications, al-
lowing for precise forecasts that support strategies for climate adaption, water resource
management, and agricultural planning.

1 Introduction
The climate has changed over the past few decades, mostly in terms of temperature and
rainfall, which has a significant effect on environmental sustainability, water resource man-
agement, and agricultural productivity. It is essential to comprehend and predict these fac-
tors in order to effectively plan and decisions-making scenarios, as well as to reduce the
hazards associated with climate oscillations (Rawat et al. 2022). Precise forecasts of rainfall
and temperature can enable decision-making by stakeholders, especially in areas that rely
significantly on climate-sensitive sectors like agriculture. Seasonal time series forecasting
is important in this setting because it helps to predict fluctuations in weather patterns, which
are significant for crop planning, disaster preparedness, and climate resilience activities.
Many researchers have developed different statistical and machine learning models for fore-
casting the integration of climatic changes.
Despite breakthroughs in climate forecasting, considerable research gaps exist. A sig-
nificant portion of the literature is based on traditional time series models such as ARIMA
(Auto Regressive Integrated Moving Average) or seasonal variants such as SARIMA (Sea-
sonal ARIMA), BATS and TBATS models, have proven effective for capturing linear trends
and seasonality (Ray et al. 2021). Dabral and Murry (2017) examined the monthly, weekly
and daily rainfall prediction using SARIMA model. The hydrological forecasts were also
estimated using a hybrid nonlinear GARCH model (Karami et al. 2024) and βSARMA
(Beta Seasonal Autoregressive Moving Average) model (Shad et al. 2023) for incorporating
seasonal dynamics and non-Gaussian behaviour. The limitation of these models lies in the
incomplete integration of exogenous variables, or outside influences, such as atmospheric
pressure, concentrations of greenhouse gases, maximum and minimum temperatures,
etc., that can affect patterns of rainfall (Shah et al. 2024). Although SARIMAX (Seasonal
ARIMA with Exogenous Regressors) incorporates external elements in an attempt to close
this gap, its efficacy is restricted in the case of highly complicated and non-linear connec-
tions (Mulla et al. 2024). The parametric and non-parametric models also used to estimate
the prediction behaviour of hydrological information (Lama et al. 2021). Also, the hybrid
approach SARIMA-GARCH model was developed using transformed hydrological data for
better prediction (Pandey et al. 2019).
In addition to these conventional statistical models, a machine learning technique also
used to forecast the pattern of climate change. The estimation of complex rainfall series
was commonly achieved using machine learning such as non-parametric time delay neural
network (TDNN) model (Benmahdjoub et al. 2013; Lama et al. 2021; Ray et al. 2023a, b).
Nonlinear autoregressive neural network (NARNN) model was also employed to assess the
forecasting nature of the nonlinear patterns and interactions of hydrological series (Rawat
et al. 2022). The evaluation of the pattern of climate changes can also be done using the
long short-term memory (LSTM) model or a distinct hybrid technique of LSTM model
(Chamatidis et al. 2023; Haq et al. 2021; Zheng et al. 2024; Coutinho et al. 2025). Ouma
et al. (2022) used satellite-based meteorological data from the Nzoia hydrologic basin to
study rainfall and runoff trend analysis using LSTM and wavelet neural network model.
The LSTM model provide more superiority than WNN as the RMSE value improved from
21 to 15 mm. A comparative analysis was done by Barrera-Animas et al. (2022) among
the modern machine learning models such as XGboost, AutoML, LSTM, Stacked-LSTM,

While extant approaches demonstrate varying degrees of forecasting efficacy, critical
methodological limitations persist. Conventional time series models (e.g., SARIMAX)
exhibit constrained capacity for modeling nonlinear interactions between climatic variables
and their exogenous drivers, while machine learning architectures (e.g., LSTM, XGBoost)
frequently lack proper uncertainty quantification mechanisms. Notably, Bayesian structural
time series (BSTS) frameworks, which inherently accommodate parameter uncertainty
through probabilistic formulations while maintaining interpretable decomposition of trend,
seasonality, and exogenous effects, remain insufficiently explored for seasonal climate
forecasting. This study addresses these methodological shortcomings through a rigorous
comparative evaluation of BSTS against both classical and machine learning paradigms,
incorporating dynamic exogenous variables while maintaining proper probabilistic founda-
tions. In literature, Bayesian structural time series (BSTS) model are employed to forecast
seasonal time series data associated with climatic variables like temperature and rainfall

tigated a two-phase Bayesian model updating and class selection approach for nonlinear
structures based on NNMs that have been established through experimentation. Molina
et al. (2025) designed an intelligent data-driven tool called Bayesian Stochastic Rainfall
Generator (BSRG) for improving the rainfall forecast. They concluded that cluster-specific
characteristics significantly influence rainfall patterns, expressed as probabilistic distribu-
tions, under both normal and extreme regimes. Wang (2009) used the Markov chain Monte
Carlo (MCMC) methods to study a comparison between non-Bayesian State Space Models
and Bayesian SSMs utilizing the parameter estimation strategy. Bayesian Structural Time
Series (BSTS) models represent a viable alternative because they combine transparency,
flexibility, and the capacity to incorporate previous information. Unlike traditional models,
BSTS do not use differencing or explicit lag structures. The Bayesian framework enables
robust quantification of uncertainty using posterior distributions, resulting in more reliable

2 Materials and Methods
2.1 Study Information
Monthly time series data for rainfall (mm), maximum temperature (°C), and minimum tem-
perature (°C) for India were collected from the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge
Portal (https://climateknowledgeportal.worldbank.org; accessed July 2024) for the period
January 1901 to December 2022. A total of 1464 observations were obtained for each
variable. The dataset covers the entire Indian subcontinent and represents area-averaged
monthly climatic conditions. The data were divided into a training set (1404 data points) and
a testing set (60 data points, corresponding to January 2017 to December 2022) for model
development and validation. Prior to splitting, data pre-processing was performed using the
Grubbs test and boxplot visualisation, which confirmed the absence of missing values and
outliers in each series (Fig. 2).
2.2 Model Description (Statistical & ML)
2.2.1 ARIMA Models

model. The autoregressive component of ARIMA is used to predict future values by exam-
ining the linear relationship between past and present observations. The parameter “p”
indicates the number of lagged observations utilized in the autoregression. ARIMA models
employ differencing to transform non-stationary time series into stationary ones, effectively
removing trends and seasonality. This differencing step is denoted by “d,” which signifies
how many times the process is applied to achieve stationarity. To handle noise and random
variations, the moving average component of ARIMA focuses on the relationship between

2.2.3 Bayesian Structural Time Series Model
The Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) model is considered more transparent than
the traditional Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) framework because it
explicitly decomposes a time series into interpretable components such as trend, seasonal-
ity, and regression effects. Unlike ARIMA models, whose representation depends on dif-
ferencing, autoregressive lags, and moving-average terms, BSTS models allow direct visual
inspection of the underlying structural components.
Another important advantage of BSTS models is their principled treatment of uncertainty.
The Bayesian framework enables quantification of posterior uncertainty associated with
individual components, incorporation of prior information, and regularization of regression
coefficients through shrinkage priors. Moreover, any ARIMA model can be represented in
an equivalent structural state-space form.

2.2.5 XGBoost Model

was formally introduced by Chen and Guestrin (2016), and has since become a widely used

data, helps in simplifying the model while maintaining accuracy. The regularization aspect
of XGBoost is particularly beneficial in time series forecasting, as it helps avoid overfitting
to noise or repetitive seasonal patterns.

an excellent choice for time series forecasting tasks. Its support for parallel and distributed

3 Results
3.1 Data Description
As standard protocol goes we start with the descriptive of the datasets being used for
the present investigation. Table 1 presents the important statistics of the datasets, which
revealed that the rainfall series having highest variation (CV % :105.927) and the maximum
temperature having lowest variation of 12.412 (CV%). The values of the kurtosis revealed
the datasets to be platykurtic in nature with rainfall and maximum temperature series being
positively skewed and minimum temperature series exhibiting negative skewness. Further,
density plots (Fig. 2) of the datasets along with the Shapiro-Wilk test statistic values distinc-
tively reveals deviation from normality. Under pre-processing of the data series, no outlier
were found, confirmed by the Grubbs test and boxplot (Fig. 2). Overall, from the descriptive
statistics and density plot we conclude that the datasets under consideration are non-normal
in nature. These characteristics guided the selection of appropriate forecasting models.

4 Discussion
The results of this study provide several important insights into the relative performance
of Bayesian, statistical, and machine learning models for seasonal climate forecasting. The
poor performance of the LSTM model across all three variables, particularly on the train-
ing set for rainfall (RMSE: 69.905; MAPE: 71.088%) is noteworthy given the widespread
enthusiasm for deep learning in climate forecasting. This result is consistent with findings
by Barrera-Animas et al. (2022), who observed that LSTM performance is highly sensitive
to data volume and hyperparameter tuning, particularly for strongly seasonal series. Pre-
processing using seasonal decomposition or deseasonalisation explored by Chamatidis et

al. (2023) may improve LSTM performance and is identified as a future research direction.
XGBoost achieved excellent training set performance across all three series (e.g., RMSE:
6.863 for rainfall; RMSE: 0.193 for maximum temperature) but exhibited pronounced over-
fitting on the testing set. For rainfall, the testing-set RMSE of XGBoost (28.571) was more
than four times greater than its training-set RMSE (6.863), a discrepancy consistent with the
well-documented tendency of gradient boosting methods to overfit when seasonal lag fea-
tures are constructed from short-memory time series (Mishra et al. 2024; Dong et al. 2023).
The inclusion of regularisation strategies or expanding the feature engineering pipeline may
help mitigate this issue. The superior generalisation of BSTS and BSTSX on the rainfall
and maximum temperature testing sets aligns with the theoretical advantages of Bayes-
ian structural modelling. As Scott and Varian (2014) examined the explicit decomposition
of trend, seasonality, and exogenous effects within a probabilistic framework reduces the
risk of overfitting while preserving interpretability. The BSTSX model incorporates maxi-
mum and minimum temperature as exogenous predictors, achieved slightly lower testing set
RMSE (25.506) than BSTS (25.804) for rainfall, confirmed that temperature variables carry
predictive information for monsoon dynamics. This finding is in agreement with Shah et al.
(2024) and Mulla et al. (2024), who highlighted the value of temperature as an exogenous
covariate in seasonal rainfall models. The negative coefficient of minimum temperature

suggested that a parsimonious linear model may be sufficient to capture its dynamics.
This finding is consistent with the view that BSTS models provide the greatest benefit
when non-linearity, structural breaks, or informative prior knowledge can be exploited
for relatively smooth, low-variance series, the classical models may remain competi-
tive (Harrison and Stevens 1971; Das and Chanda 2020). The difference between the
two models (ΔRMSE = 0.098; ΔMAPE = 0.705%) is modest, and the BSTS model retains
the advantage of uncertainty quantification through posterior distributions. Future work
employing informative priors based on climate domain knowledge may further improve
BSTS performance for this series.
The ability of BSTS and BSTSX to provide probabilistic forecasts rather than the point
estimates is particularly valuable. Probabilistic rainfall forecasts can directly inform reservoir
operations, irrigation scheduling, and drought early-warning systems. Uncertainty quantifica-
tion is especially critical in the Indian context, where the inter-annual variability of monsoon
rainfall (CV% = 105.93%) creates substantial risk for agricultural and hydrological planning.

5 Conclusion
This study modelled and forecasted the seasonal climatic variables such as rainfall, maxi-
mum temperature, and minimum temperature for India using a century-long monthly data-
set. The primary objective was to efficiently capture the seasonal pattern of the rainfall
series using temperature series as exogenous variables. Four classes of models were evalu-
ated i.e. classical statistical (SARIMA, SARIMAX), Bayesian structural (BSTS, BSTSX),
machine learning (XGBoost), and deep learning (LSTM). The LSTM model consistently
underperformed, likely owing to its limited ability to capture strong seasonal patterns in
the absence of explicit pre-processing. XGBoost exhibited pronounced overfitting, with
testing-set errors substantially exceeding training-set performance. SARIMAX established
its superiority over SARIMA by effectively incorporating exogenous temperature vari-
ables. Bayesian models (BSTS and BSTSX) demonstrated the best overall generalisation
for rainfall (testing-set MAPE: approximately 26%) and maximum temperature (testing-set
MAPE: approximately 1.59%). It should be noted that for minimum temperature, SARIMA
achieved marginally lower testing set errors than BSTS (RMSE: 0.516 vs. 0.614; MAPE:
2.230% vs. 2.935%), indicated that for low variance, autoregressive series, classical linear
models remain competitive. However, the BSTS framework retains the unique advantage
of probabilistic uncertainty quantification, which is of particular importance for appli-
cations in water resource management and agricultural planning under climate change.
Future work will investigate the performance of LSTM applied to deseasonalised series
and the incorporation of informative priors in the BSTS framework to further improve
forecast accuracy.
