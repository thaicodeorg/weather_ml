Previous researchers have employed meteorological data,
data mining techniques, and big data analysis to estimate
daily, monthly, and annual rainfall quantities [7]. To forecast
rainfall in Assam, authors Dutta and Tahbilder [8] employed
a data-mining tactic, specifically utilizing a multiple linear
regression (MLR) model. Their model incorporated maxi-
mum and minimum temperatures, wind speed, and mean sea
level variables. But they did not include wind direction in
their statistical model, which could improve prediction accu-
racy. In contrast, using tipping-bucket data, Authors Kusiak
and Verma [9] explored rainfall forecasting in a water-
shed basin near Oxford, Iowa. Their study compared five
data mining algorithms and found that the support vector
machine (SVM) outpaced the others, especially for longer
lead periods. Authors Trenberth [10] emphasized the sig-
nificance of atmospheric factors such as wind and sunlight
in influencing rainfall patterns, contributing to improved
understanding of precipitation behavior. Authors Tharun
and Prakash [11] motivated on forecasting rain severity in
Coonoor, Tamil Nadu, utilizing regression analysis and sta-
tistical modeling. By leveraging daily weather observations,
their research trained regression models, including Random
Forest, Decision Tree, and Support Vector Regression. The
Random Forest model displayed the highest performance
among the tested models.
Furthermore, using variables such as changes in mini-
mum and maximum temperatures, rainfall, wind speed, and
evaporation, authors Chowder and Girisha [12] employed
data mining methods such as clustering and classification.
Their work aimed to better understand variations in weather
and climatic characteristics, such as rainfall and humidity.
They advised using predictive analytics algorithms and high-
resolution data collection for improved precision. With the
aim of enhancing the accuracy of water demand predic-
tions, author Zhao [13] established a model to anticipate
the water demand of ecological environments, employing
tools for data visualization, data mining, predictive analy-
sis, semantic engines, storage, management, and warehous-
ing. Authors Zhou and Ruan [14] conducted a spatial risk
assessment in China’s marine transportation sector using
machine learning (ML) and geographical big data. Their
results emphasized that the XGBoost algorithm outstripped

flood vulnerability using 11 key parameters. The study also
utilized classification, regression trees, and support vector
machines (SVM) as part of its methodology. The amount
of rainfall is influenced by various factors, including cloud
cover, wind speed and direction, humidity, temperature,
water level, solar radiation, solar energy, ultraviolet index,
and other related variables [24]. A study by Nti and Zaman

Random Forest in predicting crop suitability and productiv-
ity. The models outlined here only include a small number
of variables: temperature, humidity, rainfall, pH, nitrogen,
and phosphorus. Solar radiation, visible water vapour, and
other critical elements for rainfall forecasting were identified
by [26] using a linear regression model.

Indicators
ML is a branch of artificial intelligence that learns by itself
without requiring explicit programming. Machine learning
models are vital for deriving information from data and mak-
ing automated decisions in multiple industries [34, 35]. In
this section, some of the most widely known ML algorithms
employed for building the model and evaluating its predic-

ML Algorithms
The various advanced and traditional ML models used in
this paper are described below:
AutoML
The main objective of autoML is automatic selection of the
most suitable ML framework depending on the data by algo-
rithm selection or meta-learning. This is done through the
trial of different algorithms such as SVMs, neural networks,
random forests, and decision trees, among others. AutoML
frameworks incorporate libraries of pre-implemented algo-
rithms, enabling systematic examination of various models
and configurations [36]. Recently, AutoML models such
as H2O [37], Auto-Sklearn [38], AutoTS [39], AutoGluon
[40], TPOT [41, 42], AutoKeras [43], and Auto-PyTorch
[44] have emerged, mixing feature engineering, model selec-
tion, and hyperparameter optimization. The proposed work
uses H2O, AutoKeras, and TPOT models to accurately pre-
dict rainfall. The subsequent sections review and compare
these models.
• H2O[37]: It is a distributed, open-source, and scalable
supervised learning framework within the H2O library.
It is widely used in academia and industry. It facilitates
regression, multi-class classification, and binary clas-
sification. Its key advantage lies in rapidly generating
predictions using multiple H2O models. With APIs
in various programming languages, the H2O model is
accessible and versatile across different domains. The
framework uses ML algorithms such as gradient-boost-
ing machines, generalized linear models, random forests,
and deep learning. It uses Bayesian optimization, grid,
and random search to automate model selection and
hyperparameter tuning. Thus, end-to-end automation and
interoperability across different setup formats streamline
model building and deployment, making the H2O model
useful for both beginners and professional data scientists.
• Autokeras[43]: It is an AutoML framework built on the
Keras API that prioritizes deep learning models over tra-
ditional algorithms. It utilizes a neural architecture search

• TPOT[41, 42]: This machine learning platform uses

100 pipelines. This cycle is repeated 100 times, resulting
in the top-performing models from the final generation.
Time Series Transformers
Transformer-based networks, including Autoformer,
Informer, and PatchTST is described in this section.
• Autoformer[45]: It is considered for time-series fore-
casting, particularly effective for long-term predictions
and seasonal patterns. It exploits an advanced auto-cor-
relation mechanism to capture short-term and long-term
dependencies, improving computational efficiency and
model performance. The encoder divides data from time
series into pattern and seasonal components, empowering
the model to focus on recurring patterns while filtering
out noise. The decoder progressively integrates trend
information, improving the model’s ability to detect both
short-term seasonal variations and long-term trends.
• Informer[46]: It addresses limitations of standard
Transformers by introducing ProbSparse Self-attention
for reduced memory usage and computational time, self-
attention distilling to downsample dimensions, and a gen-
erative decoder that outputs long sequences in a single
step, minimizing cumulative errors.
• PatchTST[47]: It incorporates two key features: (1)
Patching, which segments time series data into smaller
sub-sequences (patches) as input tokens, and (2) Chan-
nel Independence, treating each variable as a separate
univariate series with shared transformer weights and
embedding processes across channels.

and Zaman [25]. XGBoost has become prevalent among

Random Forest (RF)
Random Forests Algorithm is one such machine learning
algorithm that uses a combination of multiple decision
trees to leverage the benefits provided by all of them. First
introduced in [54], Random Forests can be used for both
classification and regression purposes. In the Random For-
ests model, each decision tree is constructed by randomly
choosing some features from the whole feature set and train-
ing data [55]. Random Forest combines the forecasts of all
constituent trees, usually by majority voting for classifica-
tion and averaging for regression [54, 56], thereby achiev-
ing greater predictive power and robustness than individual
decision trees. Here, different sets of predictors are used for
each training dataset to reduce variance and achieve opti-
mal results. When compared to a single decision tree, the
accuracy and robustness of the random forest prediction are
significantly improved. This is accomplished by combining
the predictions of all individual trees, which is frequently
accomplished using majority voting for classification and
averaging for regression [55, 56], as illustrated in Fig. 3,
which shows that a different set of predictors is used in each
training dataset to reduce variance and achieve good results.

Combining several weak learners into one more accurate
prediction is the goal of bagging, an ensemble method in
ML (sometimes termed Bootstrap Aggregating). Bagging
involves training multiple decision trees with different boot-
strap samples from the training data [64]. Data points could
not show up at all or many times in a single bootstrap sam-
ple. The reason is that bootstrap samples are generated by
randomly selecting a fraction of the original training data
and then replacing it. Figure 6 shows the process of the bag-
ging method.

Discussion
The methodological gaps addressed in the current study,
rather than simply comparing various models. Firstly,
compared to previous rainfall prediction research, which
heavily relies on human intervention through manual
selection of models and tuning of hyperparameters, this
research employs a fully automated AutoML approach
(H2O). This makes the study free of any human-caused
biases. Secondly, we suggest a comparative methodology
framework that would compare various machine learning
techniques, including classical models, ensemble meth-
ods, and modern transformer-based time series models
like Informer, Autoformer, and PatchTST, under the same
conditions of data and evaluation metrics. Thirdly, we
introduce a process of correlation-based feature selec-
tion before implementing any models, thereby combin-
ing statistical interpretability with automation, as previ-
ous studies have disregarded. Moreover, in addition to
other methodological contributions, the study examines
the balance between model complexity and automation,
demonstrating the effectiveness of AutoML models
against advanced transformer-based models when data
availability is limited. Lastly, the proposed framework is
specifically designed for a high-risk, data-scarce region
(Joshimath), making it practically relevant for real-world
deployment.
The parameters used in this research originate from the
visual crossing, which compiles data from local weather
stations and far-away sources like satellites and radar.
These factors are then evaluated for their potential influ-
ence on the effects of rainfall, as seen in Table 11 for the
daily rainfall forecast. Using a dummy variable construc-
tion approach, this study has taken all necessary factors
and turned non-numerical variables like icons (snow, rain,
overcast, and partly cloudy) into numeric ones. This way,
all characteristics may be employed. There is no indica-
tion of a link between the factors used by authors in [19,
76]. Liyew and Melese [77] used only three ML algo-
rithms, two performance measures (RMSE and MAE) for
assessment, and Pearson correlation to demonstrate rela-
tionships between variables; nevertheless, they neglected
to employ a grid search to enhance the model’s accuracy.
In summary, to forecast rainfall, this study analyzed all
relevant atmospheric parameters using advanced models,
including AutoML models (H2O, AutoKeras, and TPOT),
time-series transformers (Autoformer, Informer, and
PatchTST), and traditional ML models. Among these, the
AutoML model H2O demonstrated the best performance,

Conclusion
Rainfall prediction is crucial for agriculture and disaster
preparedness, helping mitigate risks from floods and rain-
related illnesses, and reducing mortality rates. This study
leverages data from the Visual Crossing platform to evaluate
advanced models, such as time series transformers (Auto-
former, Informer, PatchTST), AutoML frameworks (H2O,
AutoKeras, TPOT), and traditional ML models (Random
Forest, XGBoost, Decision Tree, Bagging, AdaBoost, Ridge
Regression, MLR, Lasso Regression, KNN). Using RMSE,

AutoML outperformed other models, eliminating the need
for manual model selection and hyperparameter tuning. Grid
search identified Random Forest as the second-best model
for rainfall forecasting, confirming its reliability in tradi-
tional ML settings.
