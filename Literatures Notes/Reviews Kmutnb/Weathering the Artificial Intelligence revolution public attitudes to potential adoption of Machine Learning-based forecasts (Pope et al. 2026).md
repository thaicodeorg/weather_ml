## Weathering the Artificial Intelligence revolution (Pope et al. 2026)

## Source Information

"Weathering the Artificial Intelligence revolution: public attitudes to potential adoption of
Machine Learning-based forecasts", Edward C. D. Pope, Kirstine I. Dale, Edward C. C. Steele and
Philippa H. R. Graham, all Met Office, Exeter, *Artificial Intelligence for the Earth Systems*,
Early Online Release, DOI 10.1175/AIES-D-25-0068.1. 24 sheets. Single-institution, four-author
paper; the Early Online Release states it "may be fully cited" and that the typeset copy will
replace it at the same DOI.

## Research Objective

Whether the UK public believes a Machine Learning Weather Prediction (MLWP) forecast is accurate
and would act on it, and how those beliefs compare with the same questions about physics-based
NWP
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=2|ABSTRACT, p.2]]).
This is the only paper in the corpus that treats ML weather prediction as an *adoption* problem
rather than a modelling problem, and for that reason it is worth a slot in the seminar even
though it contains no forecast scores at all. Its value to a forecasting seminar is as a
corrective: the technical literature in this vault is almost entirely silent on whether anyone
would use the output, and on whether labelling a forecast "AI" changes its perceived value.

## Problem

Trust in forecasts is operationally load-bearing for National Meteorological Services, because
"a lack of user trust could have serious implications for lives and livelihoods, particularly if
it undermines the effectiveness of public weather warnings"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=3|1. Introduction, p.3]]).
Existing perception research is concentrated on professional users — forecasters and domain
experts — where the evidence shows "cautious optimism about the potential of AI to enhance weather
warnings, tempered by concerns about over-reliance"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=3|1. Introduction, p.3]]).
The public-facing baseline is therefore unknown at exactly the moment MLWP is approaching
operational use. Governance pressure compounds the timing: the EU AI Act's transparency
requirements and the UK's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard are arriving while
services are still deciding how to present these systems
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=3|1. Introduction, p.3]]).

## Gap Addressed in paper

The gap is a measured public baseline for MLWP perception, collected through the Met Office's
existing Public Weather Service survey so that it can be repeated as MLWP becomes
operationally familiar
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=5|2. Public Survey of accuracy and confidence, p.5]]).
The design is deliberately instrumented as a tracking series: the paper frames the responses as
"a baseline against which we can compare changing perceptions as the public becomes more
familiar with MLWP"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=7|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.7]]).
The authors also volunteer the sharpest limitation of their own instrument in advance, which is
rare and welcome: the questions "represent a false dichotomy in that many weather forecasting
capabilities of the future will involve a blend of physics-based and machine learning components",
and absolute NWP-versus-MLWP differences "may not be directly comparable to perceptions about
something that is largely unknown"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=7|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.7]]).

## Findings and conclusion

6,022 UK adults aged 18+ were surveyed across three YouGov waves — October 2024 (2,043), January
2025 (2,011) and April 2025 (1,968)
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=6|2. Public Survey of accuracy and confidence, p.6]]).
AI is no longer unknown: 5,751 of 6,022 (over 95%) had heard of it, 1,291 could explain it in
detail, and nearly 60% could give a partial explanation
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=7|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.7]]).

The core asymmetry is large and consistent. Roughly 78% rated NWP forecasts accurate versus 45–48%
for MLWP, and the "Don't know" share is 0.5–1% for NWP against about 20% for MLWP. Confidence
behaves the same way: 80–88% confident using NWP information versus 40–44% for MLWP, with "Don't
know" at 2–4% versus roughly 17%
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=8|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.8]]).
The interpretation offered is that NWP perception is shaped by direct experience — users "know
what it does well, what it does not" — whereas MLWP perception is shaped by prior belief, and the
wider spread across groups is read as "a degree of rational scepticism"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=8|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.8]]).

Subgroup structure is where the paper earns its keep. MLWP perceptions vary far more across
groups than NWP perceptions do, and the ordering is not uniform: ABC1 respondents exceed C2DE for
both NWP and MLWP; women exceed men for NWP while men exceed women for MLWP; the South, East and
London are generally higher and the North East, Scotland and Wales lower; the 25–34 group most
often tops both; rural respondents most often bottom out
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=17|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.17]]).
The authors read this as consistent with the UK's established digital divide and as a risk of
"reinforcing existing disparities in engagement with emerging technologies".

The closing recommendations are three and are directed at the forecasting community rather than
at comms teams: **Approach** — ship integrated NWP+MLWP products rather than AI-branded ones;
**Experience** — release experimental capability to early adopters, citing the GraphCast and
FastNet publication precedent, and run consented-participant comparisons; **Evaluation** — treat
perceived accuracy and confidence as analogues of verification skill and value, and put
user-relevant metrics into training losses and performance statistics, with argument-based
assurance as the connecting framework
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=19|4. Bridging the gap, p.19]]).

## Limitations or Weakness

**The headline gap measures familiarity, not capability.** AI knowledge is itself one of the
analysed blocks, and its ordering is the strongest in the table: the maximum MLWP-confidence
group is "Heard of AI, could explain" and the minimum is "Heard of AI, could not explain"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=13|Table 1, p.13]]).
So the 47%-versus-78% contrast is substantially a literacy contrast, and reading it as a
capability assessment would be a straightforward misreading. The paper is aware that "there will
be latent factors that influence perceptions which this survey does not capture"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=11|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.11]]),
but no attempt is made to separate literacy from prior belief.

**The inferential argument is weaker than it looks.** The authors decline to compute significance
within blocks because each contains only 2 to 13 groups, and instead argue from consistency: the
median perceived accuracy is lower for MLWP in all six blocks, so under a null of no directional
difference the probability is 0.5^6
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=15|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.15]]).
The calculation is correct but the six blocks are not independent — age, gender, region, social
grade, urban/rural and AI knowledge are all correlated, and the paper cannot test that because
responses arrive pre-aggregated by group, so "individual responses are not available"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=11|3. Perceptions of accuracy and confidence, p.11]]).
The same aggregation also rules out any individual-level or multivariable analysis, which is why
the subgroup bullets are phrased as tendencies ("in general, but not uniformly") rather than
results.

**No effect sizes anywhere.** The paper reports proportions from collapsed Likert categories with
no margin of error and no confidence interval on any figure, including the subgroup dumbbells. The
top-line 78%-versus-47% gap is far larger than any plausible sampling error at n=6,022, so the
headline survives; the subgroup statements — "the South, East and London generally higher", "the
25-34 group most frequently exhibits the highest fraction" — are comparisons of extremes within
blocks of 2 to 13 groups and carry no uncertainty at all. Read them as hypotheses.

**Three waves are pooled and no per-wave result is reported.** The fieldwork spans October 2024 to
April 2025, a period of intense MLWP coverage, and the paper reports only pooled proportions. The
instability the survey is meant to track is exactly what pooling hides: attitudes may have moved
monotonically across the three waves, or reversed at the January wave. The paper's own
prediction — that users "may be willing to update their beliefs about MLWP capabilities"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=2|ABSTRACT, p.2]])
— cannot be tested from a pooled cross-section, and is instead supported by appeal to the prior
Bayesian decision-theoretic model of Pope et al. (2017), in which perception updates more slowly
for already-sceptical users
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=18|4. Bridging the gap, p.18]]).
That is a citation, not a result.

**Unweighted data is a choice, not a virtue.** Sample-to-population differences are reported as
"typically less than 1% for most groups and never exceeding 6%", and weights were deliberately
not applied "to avoid over-generalising"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=6|2. Public Survey of accuracy and confidence, p.6]]).
The argument trades a quantified bias of up to 6% in some groups against an unquantified
alternative. Since quota sampling already matches age, gender, region and social grade closely,
weighting would be the more defensible option and the unweighted results should be presented as
the sensitivity check.

**Single country, self-report, no behavioural outcome.** Everything is stated belief on a Likert
scale; no respondent made an actual decision under either forecast type, so nothing here
establishes that perception predicts behaviour. The paper is UK-only and says so, urging other
providers to repeat it "particularly in countries where training, skills and experience of AI are
very different"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ametsoc.org/aies-AIES-D-25-0068.1-weathering-the-artifical-inteligent.pdf#page=21|Closing discussion, p.21]]).

## Implication or suggestions on future research

1. Publish the three waves separately. This is the single cheapest change with the largest payoff:
   it converts a one-off snapshot into an actual trajectory, which is what the paper says the
   instrument is for.
2. Run an information-treatment arm — randomise respondents to an NWP forecast, an MLWP-labelled
   forecast, and a blended forecast with identical skill — and measure perceived accuracy after.
   That would separate label effect from ignorance effect and would put the "false dichotomy"
   caveat to work rather than leaving it as a caveat.
3. Report unweighted versus weighted results and full confidence intervals, and pre-register the
   sign test so the block-independence assumption is at least explicit.
4. Add a behavioural endpoint: a decision task with real stakes or a disclosed-stakes incentive,
   so perception can be validated against action. The 17–20% "Don't know" group is the obvious
   target, since the paper identifies it as "an important target for further research and
   engagement".
5. For the technical community, take the Evaluation recommendation literally. If subjective
   accuracy and confidence are analogues of verification skill and value, then user-relevant
   metrics belong in training losses, and the seminar's usual skill-score arguments (RMSE, ACC,
   CRPS) should be accompanied by a decision-layer argument.

## How your search can fill gap

This paper pairs directly with the two Met Office technical reports already in the corpus, and
reading the three together is the seminar's strongest available framing of the AI-adoption
question. The FastNet technical overview
([[Sources/Research Paper/arxiv.org/2509.17658v2-technical-fastnet.pdf#page=1|Abstract, p.1]])
and the AIFS-CRPS ensemble paper
([[Sources/Research Paper/Nature.com/s44387-026-00073-7-AIFS-CRPS_ensemble_forcasting_using_model.pdf#page=1|1 Introduction, p.1]])
supply the accuracy and calibration evidence the public has no way to assess; the survey shows that
roughly half the public expects AI forecasts to be less accurate than NWP and a fifth cannot say.
The natural synthesis is a permanent note on the trust-versus-skill gap: the technical case for
MLWP rests on measured skill, while the adoption case is currently decided by prior belief and AI
literacy, and the paper's own Approach recommendation — integrated NWP+MLWP products — is the
reconciliation. The companion corpus reference on the technical side is the physics-versus-AI
comparison
([[Sources/Research Paper/Wiley/Geophysical Research Letters - 2026 - Davis - Physics‐Based Versus AI Weather Prediction Models  A Comparative Performance.pdf#page=1|Abstract, p.1]]),
whose finding that skill depends on regime and variable is precisely the nuance a public
communication strategy cannot currently convey.
