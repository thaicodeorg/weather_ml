## ECMWF's societal impact through service provision, partnerships and collaborations

## Source Information

Venutia, F., C. Rabier, E. Andersson, U. Modigliani, S. English, F. Kitchena,
M. Berrone, D. Pinsault, J. Debru, M. Jarraud, 2025: ECMWF's societal impact through
service provision, partnerships and collaborations. *Journal of the European
Meteorological Society*, 2, 100013. Immutable copy:
[[Sources/Markdown/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact]].
PDF: 11 sheets.

All ten authors are ECMWF staff (Reading, Bonn) or, in three cases, ECMWF
fellows at Citizing, Paris
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=1|Affiliations]]).
This is an institutional self-assessment, and that fact governs how much weight each
claim below can carry.

*Citation convention:* *JEMS* numbers articles rather than pages (article 100013)
and this PDF carries no printed folios, so citations give the sheet and the paper's
own section number without a `p.` field.

## Research Objective

To explain how ECMWF's service provision, partnerships, collaborations and user
interaction convert its mandate — global medium-range NWP and Earth-system
monitoring for Member States — into societal benefit beyond that mandate
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=1|Abstract]]).

The AI forecasting material is a minority of the paper: one substantive passage in
section 4, a mention in section 6, and the strategy paragraph in section 7. That
proportion is stated up front because it determines what the paper can and cannot
contribute to a seminar on AI weather forecasting.

## Problem

Two problems, both institutional rather than technical. First, ECMWF's direct
consumers are 23 Member States, so the organisation must convert a scientific
mandate into a wide societal benefit through intermediaries
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=1|Abstract]]).
Second — and this is the part relevant here — the arrival of machine-learning
forecast models changes what a centre should be investing in
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=5|4. Research work]]).

## Gap Addressed in paper

The gap is a documentation gap: there is no public account of how a
specially-funded intergovernmental centre's research choices become societal
outcomes, which makes both the successes and the failures hard to learn from. The
paper's contribution is that account, including the institutional pivot to AI.

## Findings and conclusion

**ML forecast models are described as already better than NWP, entirely by
citation.** The paper states that "ML weather forecasting tools, such as Huawei's
Pangu Weather, NVIDIA's FourCastNet and Google DeepMind's GraphCast, can predict
weather with accuracy and faster than traditional NWP models in medium-range,
short-range and ensemble forecasts", citing three references and giving no metric,
no lead time, no region and no baseline
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=5|4. Research work]]).
Note the ambiguity: "with accuracy and faster" parses as *more accurately and
faster*, which is a strong claim, and the sentence as printed does not cleanly
separate the two claims.

**ECMWF's own position in the field.** AIFS runs in real time, is built on the
Anemoi toolkit, and "produces highly skilled forecasts for upper-air variables,
surface weather parameters and tropical cyclone tracks" — again with no numbers, and
again by citation to the AIFS preprint rather than to a peer-reviewed verification.
ECMWF also runs Pangu, FourCastNet and GraphCast in real time "to compare their
performances and to document their strengths and weaknesses", and is developing an
ensemble version of AIFS
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=5|4. Research work]]).
The Meta AI partnership, funded by the European Commission, began in March 2025.

**The ERA5 dependency, stated plainly and framed as an opportunity.** The paper
notes that "ML developments in weather and climate forecasting worldwide rely
heavily on the use of reanalysis data as learning datasets", and that AIFS, Pangu,
FourCastNet and GraphCast all train on ERA5 — "produced by ECMWF and funded by the
Copernicus programme". Because future ML progress depends on reanalysis
improvement, ECMWF expects ERA6 to accelerate ML's impact on the sector
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=5|4. Research work]]).

**The institutional pivot is dated.** Until 2022 ECMWF regarded AI/ML as useful
mainly for *accelerating components* of the existing NWP setup — data assimilation,
observation operators, data monitoring. It has since embraced AI/ML "also as an
alternative to physics-based simulations", with a first operational AIFS
implementation in **February 2025**, described as the first operational ML model in
the community
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=9|7. Conclusions and new ECMWF strategy]]).

**The stated strategy is hybrid, not replacement.** For 2025–2034 ECMWF intends to
stay "a leader in global NWP" and to exploit "data-driven methods **anchored on
physics-based modelling**"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=9|7. Conclusions and new ECMWF strategy]]).
Future work named: ensemble forecasting at all ranges, Earth-system extension, "the
development of a foundation model", and "developments in predicting the weather
directly from observations".

Regional diffusion is under way: ECMWF funds a MET Norway and MeteoSwiss-led
project with 12 other national meteorological services to build data-driven models
on Anemoi, so that NMHSs can train their own models on their own data
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=5|4. Research work]]).

## Limitations or Weakness

**This is a self-assessment, and its evidential weight is correspondingly low.** All
authors are the institution being assessed
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=1|Affiliations]]).
Successes are asserted; failures are not examined. There is no cost-benefit
analysis, no user survey instrument described, no quantification of the "societal
impact" that the title promises, and no independent audit. For a seminar review the
practical consequence is simple: nothing in this paper may be cited as evidence
about forecast quality.

**The one technical claim about AI forecasts carries no verification.** "More
accurate and faster than traditional NWP", applied jointly to medium-range,
short-range and *ensemble* forecasts across three models
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=5|4. Research work]]),
is the broad claim the entire literature exists to test, and it is here asserted
from three citations with no metric attached. This is precisely the failure mode
the seminar is built to catch: a superlative comparison inherited from a citation
chain rather than checked against a common reference. "Faster" is defensible on
anyone's arithmetic; "more accurate" is not a single claim at all — accuracy in
medium-range, short-range and ensemble settings is three different questions, and
each varies by variable, level, region and lead time. The Bi et al. (2023)
ProGenitor work and the AIFS preprint are cited, but no headline score from either
appears.

**The ERA5 dependency is presented as a business opportunity and not as a
verification problem.** That AIFS, Pangu, FourCastNet and GraphCast all train on
ERA5 — which ECMWF produces — is stated as a reason to invest in ERA6
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=5|4. Research work]]).
The paper does not draw the consequence that a reader of these papers needs. ERA5 is
an analysis produced by a physics-based model and data-assimilation system
assimilating observations, so it carries that system's systematic errors, and a
model trained on it inherits them. This creates three confounds the field has not
fully retired: models evaluated against ERA5-derived truth are partly evaluated
against a quantity their training data already contains; models benchmarked against
IFS share ancestry with the analysis that trained them; and the long-lead skill
ceiling of any AI model is bounded by the quality of the reanalysis it learned from,
however good its architecture is. The paper's own remedy — "predicting the weather
directly from observations"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=9|7. Conclusions and new ECMWF strategy]])
— is the correct answer to this problem, and it is listed as a distant goal rather
than recognised as the reason the dependency matters.

**"Anchored on physics-based modelling" is undefined and doing significant
unacknowledged work.** The phrase is the paper's answer to whether AI replaces NWP,
and it is the right answer, but no criterion is given for what anchoring means or
how to test it
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=9|7. Conclusions and new ECMWF strategy]]).
Three readings are possible — physics-informed constraints, physics-based
post-processing, or simply retaining IFS as a fallback — with very different
implications, and the paper does not choose between them. Since this sentence is
likely to be the most-cited line in the paper, its vagueness will propagate.

**Ensemble AI is prospect, not result.** An ensemble AIFS is "being developed"
([[Sources/Research Paper/ScienceDirect/1-s2.0-S2950630125000079-main-ECMWF-social-impact.pdf#page=5|4. Research work]]).
That matters for the seminar because deterministic skill says almost nothing about
ensemble skill, and a model that outperforms at medium range can be badly
underdispersed as an ensemble. Nothing in the paper addresses spread, calibration or
Brier/CRPS-type scores.

**No lead time, variable, region or level is ever specified for any AI claim.** For a
paper whose subject is the strategic role of AI forecasting, the entire AI
discussion is resolution-free in time and space. This is the gap the seminar's
own reading of the AIFS, FastNet and GraphCast papers exists to fill.

## Implication or suggestions on future research

The most valuable thing this paper gives the seminar is a dated, citable statement
of the institutional position from inside ECMWF: the pivot from augmenting NWP to
running an ML model operationally, in February 2025, with the strategy explicitly
keeping physics-based modelling as the anchor. That is better evidence of the field's
direction than any individual model paper, and it is the kind of primary source that
is usually missing from reviews.

Its most useful negative contribution is the ERA5 dependency, stated without
interpretation. Treating it as a verification problem rather than a supply-chain
opportunity reframes a real and under-discussed structural weakness of the whole
sub-literature: AI weather models are, at the limit, distillation of a
physics-based analysis system, and no amount of architecture will raise their
long-lead ceiling above the reanalysis that trained them. For a seminar asking
"where do ML models win and what gaps remain", this is a candidate answer to the
second half of the question that no model paper offers, because model papers are
incentivised not to raise it.

The unanswered superlative is directly usable as a research task. "More accurate and
faster than traditional NWP in medium-range, short-range and ensemble forecasts" is
three separable claims; the vault already holds the papers needed to test all
three, and the ECMWF statement gives a target to test them against. Doing so
requires only lead-time-resolved scores against a common reference, which is the
standard this seminar has been applying throughout.

The regional-diffusion programme is worth tracking for the seminar's own regional
interest. A project in which 14 national meteorological services train their own
models on their own data, coordinated through Anemoi, will generate regional
verification evidence against a *local* reference — precisely the common-reference
conditioning that the papers in this batch lacked. If those verification reports
become available, they will be more informative about real-world ML forecast skill
than most global-model papers.

## How your search can fill gap

The concrete gap is quantification. The paper asserts the field's central claim and
supplies no numbers, so the seminar's own contribution should be to assemble those
numbers: a lead-time-, variable- and region-resolved comparison of AIFS, AIFS-CRPS,
FastNet, GraphCast, Pangu-Weather and FuXi against IFS on a stated reference, using
the primary papers already in the vault. That is the "how your search can fill gap"
deliverable, and the ECMWF statement is the framing device that makes it legible.

Second, the ERA5 circularity is answerable empirically and nobody in the vault has
attempted it. Comparing a model evaluated against ERA5-derived truth against the
same model evaluated against independent station or radar observations, with
reanalysis held fixed, would quantify how much apparent skill is attributable to
shared ancestry. The Huang et al. review in this batch is directly useful here: it
trains on MODIS-derived labels and verifies against the same source, which is the
same confound in a different domain.

Third, "anchored on physics-based modelling" should be turned into a testable
taxonomy. Classifying the current physics–AI literature by which of the three
readings it implements would give the seminar a sharper instrument than the
blurring/ensembling taxonomy the literature currently uses, and would make the
ECMWF sentence evaluable rather than quotable.
